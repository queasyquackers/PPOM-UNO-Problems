Basics of Demyelination
1. Loss of myelin (demyelination)
2. Relative preservation of axons
•
Preservation of axons key to help distinguish 
demyelinating process from more nonspecific 
destructive process
•
Both an infarct and a demyelinating lesion have loss 
of myelin and show foamy macrophages 
phagocytizing the debris.
•
In an infarct, however, the axons will also be 
destroyed.
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th edition, 2014, Ch. 28

Basics of Demyelination
Effects of demyelination 
       Slowing of conduction velocity due to loss of 
saltatory conduction. 
Conduction block due to impedance mismatch between myelinated and 
unmyelinated portions of the axon.

=== PAGE 6 ===
The velocity with which an action potential travels down the axon depends 
on(a) whether the fiber is myelinated and (b) the diameter of the the fiber. 
Conduction by local current flow does occur in unmyelinated fibers. 
However, a faster method of propagation, saltatory conduction, takes place 
in myelinated fibers.

Multiple Sclerosis -  Pathogenesis

=== PAGE 10 ===
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 28
•
Available evidence: MS initiated by Th1 and Th17 T cells that react against 
myelin antigens and secrete cytokines 
•
Th1 cells secrete IFN-γ, which activates macrophages
•
Th17 cells promote recruitment of leukocytes
•
Demyelination caused by these activated leukocytes and their injurious 
products 
•
Infiltrate in plaques and surrounding regions of the brain consists of T cells 
(mainly CD4+, some CD8+) and macrophages
•
Therapies being developed that modulate or inhibit T-cell responses and 
block the recruitment of T cells to the brain
•
Contribution of humoral immunity suspected, based on oligoclonal bands of 
immunoglobulin in CSF
•
MS appears to be an autoimmune disease triggered by an environmental 
factor in a genetically susceptible individual.

Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 28
• White matter disease 
• In fresh state, lesions are firmer than surrounding white matter 
(“sclerosis”) - well circumscribed, somewhat depressed, 
glassy, gray-tan, irregularly shaped plaques
• Area of demyelination often has sharply defined borders
• Size of lesions varies from small foci (microscopic) to large 
confluent plaques 
• Plaques commonly occur adjacent to the lateral ventricles and 
also often in the corpus callosum, optic nerves and chiasm, 
brainstem, ascending and descending fiber tracts, cerebellum, 
and spinal cord.
• Plaques can extend into gray matter because myelinated 
fibers are present there as well.

MS Microscopic Pathology 
•
Active plaque -  ongoing myelin breakdown associated with abundant 
foamy macrophages; lymphocytes also present, mostly as perivascular 
cuffs, especially at outer edge of the lesion. 
•
Active lesions often centered on small veins; myelin is usually completely 
absent; axons are relatively preserved
•
In time, astrocytes undergo reactive changes. 
•
As lesions become quiescent, the inflammatory cells slowly disappear. 
•
Within inactive plaques, there is no macrophage-rich infiltrate, little to no 
myelin is found, and there is a reduction in the number of oligodendrocyte 
nuclei; instead, reactive gliosis is prominent
•
Note – Active – “acute”  /   Inactive – “chronic”
.
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th edition, 2014, Ch. 28

=== PAGE 23 ===
Multiple Sclerosis
•
Distinct episodes of neuro deficits, separated in time, attributable to white 
matter lesions separated in space
•
Common presenting symptoms:
– weakness in one or more limbs (40%); spinal cord lesions result in motor 
and sensory impairment of trunk and limbs, spasticity      
– Optic neuritis (22%) – Frequent initial manifestation: unilateral visual 
impairment due to involvement of optic nerve; (10% to 50% of patients, 
depending on population) go on to develop sensory disturbances (21%)
– diplopia (12%)
– vertigo (8%)
– bladder dysfunction (5%)
– Brainstem involvement produces cranial nerve signs (ataxia, nystagmus)
•
Symptoms may worsen when ambient temperature is higher than usual 
(Uhthoff’s phenomenon)
•
Lhermitte’s sign – electrical sensation that runs down back when bending 
neck
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th edition, 2014, Ch. 28, 
and 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 28

=== PAGE 24 ===
Multiple Sclerosis
• CSF abnormalities: mildly elevated protein levels, moderate 
pleocytosis 1/3 cases (increase mononuclear wbcs), 
increased IgG levels, and oligoclonal IgG bands 
(immunoelectrophoresis) - presence of a small number of 
activated B-cell clones in the CNS, thought to be self-reactive
• Evoked response testing may detect slowing in or interruption 
of conduction in visual, auditory, somatosensory or motor 
pathways.
• Magnetic resonance imaging – to follow disease progression
• Treatment consists of several types of immunosuppressive or 
immunomodulatory agents; may slow the progression of the 
disease but not curative
• Key to diagnosis is recognizing signs and symptoms that 
would represent multiple lesions, separated by time and in 
different locations.
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th edition, 2014, Ch. 28, and 10th edition, 
2020, Ch. 28

The diagnosis of MS cannot be made only on the basis of MR imaging. 
Other neurologic diseases cause lesions in the brain that appear similar.
Normal MR does not absolutely rule out a diagnosis of MS. 
Approximately 5% of patients who are confirmed to have multiple 
sclerosis on the basis of other criteria, do not show any plaques or 
lesions in the brain on MR images. 
MRI is preferred method 
of imaging the brain to 
detect plaques or 
scarring caused by MS. 
Often, brains that appear 
to be normal on CT 
images will show MS 
plaques on MRI

=== PAGE 31 ===
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 27
• Inflammation of peripheral nerves, perivenular and endoneurial 
infiltration by lymphocytes, macrophages, and few plasma cells. 
• Segmental demyelination affecting peripheral nerves most 
prominent lesion; if severe - damage to axons but mostly 
preserved, Schwann cell preservation
• EM shows early effect on myelin sheaths: cytoplasmic 
processes of macrophages penetrate basement membrane of 
Schwann cells, especially near nodes of Ranvier, and extend 
between the myelin lamellae, stripping the myelin sheath from 
the axon. 
• Ultimately, the remnants of the myelin sheath are engulfed by 
the macrophages. Inflammation and demyelination can be 
widespread in the peripheral nervous system but are most 
prominent proximally, close to the nerve roots.

=== PAGE 34 ===
Guillain-Barre Syndrome- Clinical
• Ascending paralysis and areflexia 
• Deep tendon reflexes disappear early 
• Sensory involvement, (loss of pain sensation), often present but 
prominent
• Nerve conduction velocities slowed (multifocal destruction of 
myelin segments in many axons within a nerve)
• Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein levels elevated due to 
inflammation and altered permeability of microcirculation within 
spinal roots as they cross subarachnoid space
• Inflammatory cells remain confined to roots; results in little or no 
CSF pleocytosis
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 35 ===
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 27
• Many patients spend weeks in the intensive care unit 
(ICU) before recovering normal function
• With improved supportive respiratory care, 
cardiovascular monitoring, and prophylaxis against deep 
venous thrombosis, the mortality rate has fallen
• Plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulin 
therapy helpful; they remove pathogenic antibodies and 
suppress immune function 
• 2% to 5% of patients die of respiratory paralysis, 
autonomic instability, cardiac arrest, or related 
complications
• Up to 20% of hospitalized survivors suffer long-term 
disability

Leukodystrophies (genetic metabolic 
disorders)
“Dysmyelinating” diseases; abnormality in formation or 
stability of myelin, rather than process in which normal 
myelin is damaged. 
Characteristics of leukodystrophies:
Insidious and progressive loss of cerebral function
• Age: infancy through adolescence 
• Lysosomal or peroxisomal enzyme defects 
• Involve both CNS and the PNS 
• Progressive, resulting in death at early age 
• Generally, the earlier the onset of the disease the 
more severe clinically. 
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th edition, 2014, Ch. 28

Metachromatic Leukodystrophy 
•
Autosomal recessive disease  - deficiency of lysosomal enzyme, 
arylsulfatase A, which cleaves sulfate from sulfate-containing lipids 
(sulfatides) as first step in their degradation
•
Leads to accumulation of sulfatides, especially cerebroside sulfate, toxic to 
white matter. 
•
Histologically - demyelination with resulting gliosis
•
Macrophages with vacuolated cytoplasm are scattered throughout white 
matter
•
Membrane-bound vacuoles contain complex crystalloid structures made of 
sulfatides; when they bind to certain dyes (e.g.,toluidine blue) sulfatides shift  
absorbance spectrum of dye, known as metachromasia 
•
Similar metachromatic material detected in peripheral nerves and in urine – 
urine test sensitive method for diagnosis.
•
Multiple clinical subtypes 
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 28

=== PAGE 40 ===
Krabbe Disease (Globoid Cell 
Leukodystrophy) 
• Autosomal recessive leukodystrophy  - deficiency of 
galactocerebroside β-galactosidase, enzyme 
needed for catabolism of galactocerebroside to 
ceramide and galactose
• Because of impaired catabolism, alternative catabolic 
pathway shunts galactocerebroside to 
galactosylsphingosine
• Elevated levels of glactosylsphingosine toxic to 
oligodendrocytes and astrocytes
• Brain shows loss of myelin and oligodendrocytes and 
also affects peripheral nerves (loss of Schwann cells) 
• Diagnositic feature – aggregation of engorged 
macrophages (globoid cells) in brain and around blood 
vessels 
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10 th edition, 2020, Ch. 28

=== PAGE 42 ===
Adrenoleukodystrophy 
• X-linked recessive disease: loss-of-function mutations in 
ABCD1 (encodes an ATP-binding cassette transporter protein 
required for transport of fatty acids into peroxisomes)
• Boys present with behavioral changes and adrenal 
insufficiency 
• Loss of ABCD1 leads to inability to catabolize very-long-chain 
fatty acids within peroxisomes, resulting in elevated serum 
levels of VLCFAs
• Symptoms due to progressive CNS, peripheral nerve, and 
adrenal disease
• CNS myelin loss, gliosis and extensive lymphocytic 
infiltration 
• Adrenal cortical atrophy results in hypocortisolism.
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th edition, 2020, Ch. 28

Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy
• Immunosuppressed patients, including 
transplant patients and AIDS patients, are at 
risk. Immunocompetent people usually do not 
get PML
• Clinical – focal (depending on

signs

cerebellar demyelinative lesions can be seen 
on CT and MRI

PML
• Patches of irregular, ill-defined destruction of 
white matter
• Micro: 
– patches of demyelination, often subcortical 
– In center: usually lipid laden macrophages and 
decreased number of axons
– Edge of lesion - very enlarged oligodendrocyte 
nuceli; containing glassy amphophilic viral inclusion
– Astrogliosis, include atypical forms (bizarre, giant 
astrocytes)
