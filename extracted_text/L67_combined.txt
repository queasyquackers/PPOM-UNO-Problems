Opioid Receptors
• δ: analgesia, anxiety/mood
• μ : analgesia, euphoria, physical 
dependence, bradycardia, GI, respiratory 
depression, and miosis 
• Κ: analgesia, sedation, hallucinations, 
dissociation, and dysphoria.

Opioids: Gi Receptor Mechanism

--- Page 9 ---
Pharmacokinetics
• Well absorbed, rapid distribution.
• Highly lipophilic
• First pass effect
• Glucuronidation to metabolites: morphine-6-glucuronide, 
normeperidine. 
• Renal excretion
• Patient controlled analgesia (PCA) and spinal delivery
--- Page 10 ---
Analgesia
• Strong agonists: morphine, methadone, heroin, 
meperidine, fentanyl
• Mixed agonist-antagonist: buprenorphine
• Weak (or partial) agonist: codeine, hydrocodone 
(often combined with COX inhibitor).
--- Page 11 ---
Cardio-Respiratory
• Vasodilation= hypotension
• Reduction of myocardial oxygen demand, cardiac work.
• Decrease PVR, preload, afterload 
• Cause of death in overdose =Respiratory depression
• Opioids decrease the sensitivity of the medulla to 
increased carbon dioxide.
• Increase intracranial pressure (worsen head injury 
outcome).

• Constriction of spinchter of Oddi, urinary spinchter

Gastrointestinal

• Parasympathetic effect
• Stimulation of the Edinger-Westphal nucleus of oculomotor

• Miosis is characteristic of the use and abuse of the opioids.
• No tolerance is observed (diagnostic).

• Histamine release (pruritis, bronchospasm, vasodilation)
• Increased prolactin release
• Inhibition of LH, FSH release
• Suppress immunity
• Adjunctive in anesthesia (Alfentanil, Sulfentanil, Remifentanil). 
Provides sedation and analgesia

• The fetus may become physically dependent in utero and 
manifest withdrawal symptoms early postpartum 
• Withdrawal symptoms in the infant: irritability, shrill 
crying, diarrhea, or seizures.
• More severe withdrawal: camphorated tincture of opium 
(previous). Current: Morphine, methadone
• Phenobarbital and clonidine (adjunctive use)
Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome
--- Page 16 ---
• Occurs with all opioids
• Receptor uncoupling and 
degradation (fewer receptors on 
surface)
• Dysfunction of structural interactions 
between the μ receptor G proteins, 
second-messenger systems
• No tolerance to miosis or constipation
• This (and dependence) should not be 
used as a barrier to providing patients 
with the best possible care and quality 
of life.

Tolerance: Changing Theories

--- Page 17 ---
• Occurs 6-12 h after last dose, continues for several days, and ends 
within 72 h.
• Symptoms: Rhinorrhea, sweating, lacrimation, chills, gooseflesh, 
yawning, anxiety, aggression, mydriasis
• Drugs with a long t1/2, produce a prolonged withdrawal 
(Methadone)
• Opioids bind in the nucleus accumbens, release dopamine leading 
to addiction “reward center”
Opioid Withdrawal
--- Page 18 ---
• Increase the sensation of pain
• Activation of the bradykinin and NMDA receptors 
are likely causes.
• Discourage opioids for chronic pain
Hyperalgesia
--- Page 19 ---
• “Standard” opioid agonist
• Reacts with all opioid receptors
• Uses: moderate-severe pain, reduces dose of anesthetics, and 
anxiolytic effect.
• MI: produces vasodilation
• Hypersensitivity in respiratory disease
• Respiratory depression (fatal)
• Not for head trauma patients
• Intense sedation and respiratory depression when combined 
with other sedatives, alcohol, or barbiturates.
Morphine
--- Page 20 ---
• Mild-moderate pain; metabolized to morphine. Considered to have 
“partial” activity.
• Combined with non-opioids to relieve pain.
• Antitussive 
• Codeine undergoes CYP2D6 metabolism to morphine. Genetic 
polymorphisms may increase effects. 
• “Ultra-fast metabolizer” is a concern.
Codeine
--- Page 21 ---
• Rapid onset-short duration of action.
• Normeperidine (metabolite) can induce CNS stimulation.
• Serotonin syndrome with SSRIs, linezolid, tricyclic antidepressants, 
“triptan” drugs for migraines. 
• Not with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) 
• Anti-muscarinic effects (mydriasis, tachycardia)
• Some local anesthetic effects
Meperidine
--- Page 22 ---
• SSRIs, TCAs, meperidine, dextromethorphan, St. John’s Wort, 
linezolid, “triptans”
• Symptoms: rigidity, hyperthermia, myoclonus, delirium, 
hypertension, tachycardia within 24 hrs, unlike neuroleptic 
malignant syndrome (weeks to months). Also, GI effects
• Treatment: Benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine, ventilation 
Serotonin Syndrome
--- Page 23 ---
• Antidiarrheal
• Atropine reduces abuse potential 
• Loperamide: derivative of diphenoxylate.  Does not 
cross BBB.
Diphenoxylate
--- Page 24 ---
• 80-100x more potent than morphine; transdermal or 
transmucosal. Lipophilic
• Every 3 days (patch)
• Lozenges to induce anesthesia in children.
• Contraindicated in pregnancy
Fentanyl
--- Page 25 ---
• L-isomer of morphine
• Moderate to severe pain
• D-isomer (Dextromethorphan; DM). Not addictive 
cough suppression at normal doses. 
oNMDA receptor antagonist
oSERT and NET reuptake inhibition
Levorphanol
--- Page 26 ---
• Like morphine, but has better bioavailability 
and long duration (t1/2= 15-40h)
• Treats heroin addiction.
• CYP 3A4 substrate
• NMDA receptor antagonist
• Moderate to severe pain
Methadone
--- Page 27 ---
• Injected, snorted, adulterated.
• Chronic injection leads to collapse of vessels 
• Highly lipophilic
• Metabolized to morphine ~diacetylmorphine
• Pregnancy: newborn addicted to heroin, withdrawal.
• Increased incidence of HIV and hepatitis (needle sharing)
• Oxycontin®- gave a “heroin like” effect when crushed and 
snorted. New formulations provide special coatings to prevent 
crushing. 
Heroin: Schedule I
--- Page 28 ---
• µ receptor agonist and reuptake inhibitor of NE and 5HT
• Can precipitate withdrawal. 
• CYP2D6 polymorphism “ultra-fast metabolism”
• Used for chronic pain (neuropathic)
• Combined with acetaminophen/NSAIDs
• Tapentadol (µ receptor agonist and reuptake inhibitor of NE, 
minimal effect on 5-HT). 
• Both have serotonin syndrome warning.
Tramadol
--- Page 29 ---
• Treats opioid-induce constipation.
• Does not cross blood brain barrier (BBB). Quaternary 
ammonium cation
• Alvimopan: treats postoperative ileus. Antagonist of of 
mu receptor in GI tract. Contraindicated in patients who 
had opioids within 7 days; do not give alvimopan for 
more than 7 days. MI risk increased.
Methylnaltrexone
--- Page 30 ---
• Pentazocine, Butorphanol, Nalbuphine
• Retains analgesia with reduced addiction potential (ĸ > µ 
receptor). 
• Less physical dependence and respiratory depression.
• Psychomimetic effects (ĸ receptor). Pentazocine
• Precipitate withdrawal in morphine addicted patient.
Mixed Agonist-Antagonist
--- Page 31 ---
• Partial µ agonist.  At higher doses is an antagonist because of K receptor 
blockade
• Slow dissociation from µ receptor.
• CYP 3A4 substrate
• Sublingual, transdermal, parenteral
• Adverse effects are similar to morphine and pentazocine
• Treats opioid dependence
• Precipitate withdrawal in opioid users
• Buprenorphine and naloxone (Suboxone®)
Buprenorphine
--- Page 32 ---
• Naloxone: IV; fast onset for opioid 
overdose (1-3 min). Rapid offset. 
Repeated administration until opioid is 
cleared.
• Approved in neonates with respiratory 
depression born to heroin mothers
• Naltrexone: oral; long-duration. 
Maintenance therapy. Reduces alcohol 
cravings. Combined with bupropion for 
weight loss potential.
Opioid Antagonists

--- Page 33 ---
• Heroin: Rapid tolerance and dependence
• MOA: μ  and κ receptor agonism
• Overdose: IV naloxone and ventilation
• Symptoms: “everything is down”, stupor, bradycardia, decreased 
respirations, miosis.
• Withdrawal: Methadone or buprenorphine
• Symptoms: lacrimation, rhinorrhea, yawning, sweating, gooseflesh, 
tremors.
