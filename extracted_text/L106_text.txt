SOFT TISSUE TUMORS
•
Malignant soft tissue tumors incidence is less that 1% of all cancers; causes 2% of cancer 
mortality – usually aggressive
•
Benign > malignant; 100 to 1  (except for skeletal muscle neoplasms)
•
Most occur in extremities, esp. thigh; 15% arise in children
•
Commonly present as a growing mass, sometimes painful, possible loss of range of motion
•
Most sporadic; minority associated with mutations in tumor suppressor genes associated 
with syndromes (ex., Neurofibromatosis 1, Gardner syndrome, Li-Fraumeni, etc)
•
Some linked to environment – radiation, burns, toxins
•
Most appear to arise from pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells – acquire somatic “driver” 
mutations
•
20% have simple karyotypes -  e.g., Synovial sarcoma,  Ewing sarcoma
•
80% have complex karyotypes – multiple chromosomal gains and losses, e.g., 
undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma
•
Pathological classification combines morphology, IHC, and molecular diagnostics; grade and 
stage – important prognostic indicators
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

LIPOMA
• Benign tumor of fat (adipose tissue); most common soft 
tissue tumor in adults; rare variants
• Well-encapsulated mass of mature adipocytes; usually in 
subcutis of proximal extremities and trunk during middle 
adulthood
• Infrequently: Large, intramuscular, and poorly 
circumscribed
• Lipomatosis: Multifocal lipomas involving a limb
• Most are soft, mobile, painless, cured by simple excision
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

LIPOSARCOMA
•
Malignant tumors of adipose tissue; most common sarcomas of adulthood
•
Usually in deep soft tissues of proximal extremities and retroperitoneum of 6th and 7th decades of life 
with pain, soft, swelling, fatigue, decreased of range of motion
•
Three distinct subtypes: well-differentiated, myxoid, and pleomorphic; have different genetic 
aberrations
–
Well-differentiated liposarcomas have amplifications of chromosome region 12q13–q15, which 
includes the p53 inhibitor MDM2
–
Myxoid liposarcoma have fusion gene generated by a t(12;16) translocation, arrests adipocyte 
differentiation, leading to unregulated proliferation of primitive cells
–
Pleomorphic liposarcomas have complex karyotypes without reproducible genetic abnormalities
•
Well-differentiated liposarcoma: Mature adipocytes with scattered atypical spindle cells 
•
Myxoid liposarcoma: Abundant basophilic extracellular matrix, arborizing capillaries, and primitive cells 
at various stages of adipocyte differentiation
•
Pleomorphic liposarcoma: Pleomorphic spindle cells of high grade sarcoma, and anaplastic cells with 
bizarre nuclei mixed with occasional immature adipocytes (lipoblasts), areas of necrosis and 
hemorrhage, containing less fatty tissue than well-diferentiated, usually well demarcated but not 
encapsulated; stains for S-100, negative for keratins, desmin.
•
Recur locally, unless adequately excised
•
Well-differentiated tumors – indolent; pleomorphic liposarcomas – aggressive, frequently metastasize; 
myxoid tumors behavior intermediate
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

NODULAR FASCIITIS
• Self-limited fibroblastic and myofibroblastic proliferation, 
occurs in young adults; most often forearm, chest, and 
back; feels firm, fixed, rubbery, and solid, may be tender 
or painful to touch, rapid growth over weeks to months
• History of trauma in 10% to 50% of cases, (once thought to 
be reactive)
• Clonal proliferation with  t(17;22) translocation - produces 
a MYH9-USP6 fusion
• Unknown why neoplastic cells do not become malignant
• Usually regresses spontaneously; if excised, rarely recurs
• Deep dermis, subcutis, fascia, or muscle
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

=== PAGE 16 ===
Nodular Fascitis
• Gross: non-encapsulated lesion, well-circumscribed or slightly 
infiltrative, < 3 cm 
• Histo: Plump, immature-appearing fibroblasts and 
myofibroblasts with elongated nuclei showing punctate 
nucleoli; frequent mitoses – none abnormal
• Changes from hypercellular regions with myxoid stroma to 
hypocellular areas with fibrous stroma (zonation)
• Storiform or fascicular patterns, common in cellular areas; 
metaplastic bone, cystic areas, prominent vessels, 
extravasated red cells, and infiltrating lymphocytes common
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

Superficial Fibromatoses
•
Benign growth - can cause local deformity, painless, firm, subcutaneous induration, 
innocuous clinical course
•
M > F males
•
Nodular, proliferative lesions made of plump spindle cells (fibroblasts) arranged in poorly 
defined long fascicles surrounded by abundant dense collagen
•
Clinical subtypes:
– Dupuytren contracture  (palmar fibromatosis):  Irregular or nodular thickening of 
superficial palmar fascia, unilateral or bilateral; increased incidence with age; 
attachment to overlying skin: puckering or dimpling, slowly progressive flexion 
contracture
–  Ledderhose disease (plantar fibromatosis): In boys < 10 years of age into adolescence; 
unilateral, does not cause contractures (may be associated with palmar and penile 
fibromatosis)
– Peyronie disease: (penile fibromatosis): Palpable induration or mass on dorsolateral 
aspect of penis; may cause abnormal curvature of the shaft and constriction of the 
urethra
•
Palmar and plantar progress in about 50% of cases; some resolve spontaneously; recurrence 
is common (even after excision)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

Deep Fibromatoses (Desmoid Tumors)
• Large (avg. ~ 10 cm), infiltrative masses, frequently recur but do not 
metastasize; teens to 30s, mostly women
• Abdominal fibromatosis usually arises in musculoaponeurotic 
structures of anterior lateral abdominal wall; may also be in limb 
girdles or mesentery
• Deep fibromatoses contain mutations in the APC or CTNNB1 (β-
catenin) genes, both lead to increased Wnt signaling
• Majority have sporadic CTNNB1 mutations (note: individuals with 
familial adenomatous polyposis (germline APC mutations, Gardner 
syndrome) are predisposed to deep fibromatosis)

=== PAGE 21 ===
Deep Fibromatosis
• Gray-white, firm, poorly demarcated masses from 1 to 15 cm
• Rubbery, tough, fixed, infiltrate surrounding muscle, nerve, and fat
• May be disfiguring or disabling, painful
• Micro:  Bland fibroblasts arranged in long parallel fascicles amid 
dense collagen; no cellular; atypia; no mitoses; resembles scar tissue
• Lesions are infiltrative, complete excision difficult, local infiltration 
into adjacent muscle; do not spread to other areas of the body (but 
they can be very aggressive, acting more like cancers and growing 
into nearby structures and organs, so are often cared for by cancer 
doctors)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10 th ed. 2020, Ch.26

=== PAGE 23 ===
Fibrosarcoma
•
Rare, malignant tumor of fibroblasts (<3% of adult sarcomas); most patients 40 - 55 years
•
Two types of fibrosarcomas: infantile/congenital-type fibrosarcoma (rarely mets), and adult-
type fibrosarcoma
•
Many cases formerly called fibrosarcoma where actually dedifferentiated liposarcoma, 
fibromatosis, fibrosarcomatous DFSP, low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, MPNST, synovial 
sarcoma or MFH-pleomorphic
•
Usually deep soft tissue of lower extremities or trunk, painful, fixed, soft tumor, 
unexplained weight loss
•
50% recur, 25% metastasize (lung, bone); more metastases if more cellular and higher 
mitotic activity
•
Survival: 5 year - 41%, 10 year - 29%
•
Diagnosis of exclusion: IHC and molecular studies identify subtypes
•
May appear well circumscribed but non-encapsulated; fleshy, hemorrhagic, necrotic, white-
tan
•
Histo: Highly cellular fibroblastic proliferation in herringbone pattern and variable collagen; 
scant cytoplasm, tapering elongated dark nuclei with increased granular chromatin, variable 
nucleoli; mitotic activity present, often with abnormal forms, stains positive for vimentin, 
negative for S-100, cytokeratins
•
Treatment : Radical excision, radiation if residual tumor or positive margins; possibly 
chemotherapy if high grade
•
Definitive cause not identified: Variable genetic mutations; predisposing factors-sites of prior 
injury, chronic inflammation, irradiated tissue, preexisting well-differentiated liposarcoma

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS)
•
Rare malignant mesenchymal tumor with skeletal muscle differentiation (But most common soft tissue sarcoma of 
childhood, cancer in the connective tissue wherein the mesenchymal-derived malignant cells resemble the primitive 
developing skeletal muscle, usually starts in muscle tissue)
•
Four subtypes are recognized: alveolar (20%), embryonal (50%), pleomorphic (20%)
•
Generally considered to be a disease of childhood, as the vast majority of cases occur in those below the age of 18
•
Symptoms vary depending on the tumor’s location, commonly a quickly growing mass with pain, unexplained weight 
loss
•
Alveolar and embryonal subtypes: Most common soft tissue sarcomas of childhood and adolescence, usually before 
20 years of age (alveolar, older children and young adults, 20-35 years-old, arms, legs, or trunk)
–
Pediatric rhabdomyosarcomas often occur in the sinuses, head and neck, and genitourinary tract
•
Pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma usually occurs in adults (typically affects adults ages 50 and older); spindle 
cell/sclerosing type affects all ages, most common locations lower and upper limbs
•
Aggressive and usually treated by surgical resection and chemotherapy, with or without radiation therapy
•
Histologic type and location correlated with survival; sarcoma botryoides (subtype of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma) 
has the most favorable prognosis with good results from treatment; alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma four year survival 
rate without remission for local tumors is 65 percent, while the four year survival rate with metastatic is only 15%; 
pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma often fatal
•
Stains positive for desmin, myogenin, negative for S-100, cytokeratins. Presence of the PAX-FOXO1 fusion gene that is 
found in some patients with RMS is associated with poorer survival (due to t(2;13) or t(1;13) translocations, PAX3 is a 
transcription factor that initiates skeletal muscle differentiation, PAX3-FOXO1 fusion protein interferes with 
differentiation)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed, 2020, Ch.26

Synovial sarcoma
•
The most common location for these tumors is within the deep soft tissues 
adjacent to large joints, e.g. knee and popliteal fossa
•
Can present in locations that lack synovium; uncertain origin, inconsistent with 
origin from synoviocytes or synovial structures, e.g. joints, tendon sheaths and 
bursae
•
Approximately 10% of all soft tissue sarcomas, usually 20s to 40s
•
Individuals often present with deep-seated pain, numbness, limited range of 
motion, firm, fixed, lump or swelling under the skin, often around the joints in the 
arms or legs
•
Symptoms similar to those of arthritis or bursitis, such as swelling and 
inflammation present for several years
•
Characteristic chromosomal translocation t(x;18)(p11;q11): Produces fusion of 
SS18 gene to one of three SSX genes -  fusions encode chimeric proteins that 
interfere with normal chromatin remodeling (the repositioning of nucleosomes on 
chromatin that influences gene expression)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

=== PAGE 34 ===
Synovial Sarcoma
•
Micro: Monophasic or biphasic 
•
Monophasic - uniform spindle cells with scant cytoplasm and dense chromatin arranged in short, 
tightly packed fascicles and biphasic also contains gland-like structures composed of cuboidal to 
columnar epithelioid cells
•
IHC helpful because tumor cells, especially in biphasic type, are positive for epithelial antigens 
versus most other sarcomas (e.g., cytokeratins, epithelial component is consistently positive for CK7, 
CK8, CK14, CK18, CK19; spindle cell component usually focal and variably positive for CK7, CK19, CK18, 
CK8, CK14, CK17, CK20)
•
Treated aggressively with limb-sparing surgery and chemotherapy
•
5-year survival varies from 25% to 62%,  related to tumor stage and patient age
•
In general, synovial sarcoma has a survival rate of 50-60% at 5 years and 40-50% at 10 years
•
The survival rate for synovial sarcoma is lower in cases where the cancer has returned or spread
•
Treatment involves surgery, chemotherapy and radiation. Some factors for prognosis include the 
following: Small tumor size—less than 5cm wide, few mitoses, easy to remove by surgery
•
Common sites of metastases are the lung and, unusually for sarcomas, regional lymph nodes
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26

=== PAGE 36 ===
Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma
•
Include malignant mesenchymal tumors with high-grade, pleomorphic cells that cannot be 
classified into another category by histomorphology, immunophenotype, and genetics (old 
name malignant fibrous histiocytoma)
•
Most arise in the deep soft tissues of extremities, especially thigh of middle-aged or older 
adults as a growing fixed, firm, lump or area of swelling. If it grows very large, there may be 
pain, tingling and numbness.
•
Usually large, gray-white fleshy masses; can grow to 20 cm
•
Hemorrhage common
•
Micro:  Extremely pleomorphic, composed of sheets of large, anaplastic spindled to 
polygonal cells with hyperchromatic irregular, sometimes bizarre nuclei; mitotic figures, 
including atypical asymmetric forms abundant
•
By definition, tumor cells lack both differentiation along recognized lineages 
phenotypically (stains) and characteristic genetic defects
•
Aggressive malignancies: Treated with surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation; 
prognosis is generally poor. Metastases occur in 30% to 50% of cases
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of  Disease, 10th ed. 2020, Ch.26
