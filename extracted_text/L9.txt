Nuclei of the Reticular Formation
Diffuse projection system: 
It is estimated that a single neuron in this 
network may have synapses with as 
many as 25,000 other neurons.
Projection neurons release the 
following neurotransmitters to 
send diffuse, modulatory inputs 
to cerebral cortex, thalamus, 
hypothalamus and spinal cord:
•
Serotonin (5-HT)
•
Norepinephrine 
(NE)/epinephrine
•
Dopamine (DA) 
•
Acetylcholine (ACh)
•
Histamine

=== PAGE 8 ===
Serotonergic System
Cell bodies (nuclei):
• Raphe nuclei: along the entire brainstem
• dorsal raphe nuclei (midbrain), project to the forebrain 
• raphe magnus nucleus (medulla), project to the brainstem and spinal cord 
Functions: 
• Arousal & selective attention (reticular activating system, RAS)
• Sleep
• Sensory processing, e.g., pain
• Regulation of muscle tone & segmental spinal reflexes, e.g., reticulospinal system

• Locus coeruleus, project to 
all parts of the CNS 
• Lateral tegmental area
Functions: 
• Arousal & selective attention 
(reticular activating system, 
RAS)
• Sleep
• Sensory processing, e.g., pain
• Autonomic functions

=== PAGE 11 ===
Cholinergic System
Functions: 
• Learning & memory, cognition - NBM 
(Alzheimer’s Disease, Parkinson’s 
Disease)
• Arousal (RAS) and selective attention 
• Locomotion - PPT (fixed gait in PD)
Cell bodies:
• Nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM, basal 
forebrain system), project to the entire cortex
• Pedunculopontine tegmental nuclei (PPT), 
project to brainstem and spinal cord
• NBM & PPT activate cortical neurons via 
thalamic neurons

Coma: is marked by
•
Low arousal, low responsive state 
•
Absence of eye, verbal or motor responses
•
Abnormal EEG 
•
No sleep-wake cycle
•
Needs respiration support 
•
Requires extensive lesions of 
– ARAS (most often) or
– Both cerebral hemispheres (less common)
•
Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): simple and reliable to gauge of 
the severity

=== PAGE 20 ===

*Moderate Head injury = 9-13

=== PAGE 21 ===
Persistent vegetative state 
(“unresponsive wakefulness”) is marked by 
moderate arousal and low awareness.  
• Respond to external stimuli, such as food and 
touch  
• Eyes open and close  
• Intact sleep-wake cycle
• Capable of swallowing  
• Sometimes show very simple behaviors, teeth 
grinding, eye tracking, but do not show 
external evidence of consciousness
• Do not need artificial respiration
• Rarely return to normal consciousness if 
prolonged by more than a year
Terry Schiavo (1963-2005)

=== PAGE 23 ===
Minimally conscious state (new diagnosis) is 
marked by 
• Moderate arousal and intermittent awareness.  
• Similar to persistent vegetative state but there are 
occasional external indications of consciousness. 
• Sometime response to instructions, some language, 
some appropriate recognition of objects.  
• More consistent and steady improvement compared 
to persistent vegetative state.  
• Rare but possible full recovery.

=== PAGE 24 ===
Locked-in syndrome is marked by 
•
ARAS is spared - full arousal and full awareness, but no voluntary movement aside 
from eye movements.  
•
Bilateral artery stroke: Dense pontine lesion severing motor tracts  
•
Intact sensory transmission
•
Can use various devices to communicate (e.g. triggered by blinking, or brain-waves)
•
Permanent
The Diving Bell and the Butterfly
* Contrast with ALS, death of upper motor neurons

EEG is associated with different stages of sleep and wakefulness
•
Awake
•
Awake with eyes open: high frequency, 
low amplitude beta (β) waves
•
Relaxed wakefulness (eyes shut) shows 
rhythmic 8–12-Hz alpha (α) waves
•
NREM (Non-REM) sleep 
•
Stage N1 shows mixed frequencies, 
especially 3–7-Hz theta (θ) waves
•
Stage N2 shows 12–14-Hz sleep spindles 
and K-complexes
•
Stage N3 (slow wave sleep) shows large-
amplitude (>75 μV) 0.5–2-Hz delta (δ) 
waves. 
•
REM (Rapid Eye Movement) sleep 
•
shows low-amplitude, mixed frequencies 
with saw-tooth waves, close to beta (β)

Neurochemistry of Sleep
•
Neurotransmitters promoting awakefulness: 
•
Glutamate: universal 
•
Serotonin neurons: Raphe nuclei
•
Norepinephrine neurons: Locus coeruleus
•
Acetylcholine neurons: basal forebrain and dorsal tegmentum
•
Histamine neurons: Tuberomammillary nucleus
•
Hypocretin (Orexin) neurons: Lateral hypothalamus
•
Narcolepsy: low hypocretin in the brain

Descending Pain Modulation
• Stimulation of periaqueductal gray 
matter (PAG) excites locus 
coeruleus (NE), and rostral 
ventromedial medulla (RVM) to 
inhibit pain
• These project to the dorsal horn 
(substantia gelatinosa) and 
activate enkephalin-containing 
interneurons
• PAG receives input from 
spinomesencephalic tract, i.e. 
ascending pain information drives 
descending pain inhibition
• PAG has abundant opioid receptors.
• Chronic neuropathic pain patients 
engage this pathway → descending 
pain facilitation
vlPAG: ventrolateral PAG
LC: locus coeruleus
SRD: subnucleus reticularis dorsalis 
RVM: rostral ventromedial medulla
SpV: spinal trigeminal nucleus 
DH: dorsal horn
PTN: painful trigeminal neuropathy
Mills et al., J. Neurosci, 2018,38(2):465
https://www.jneurosci.org/content/jneuro/38/2/465.full.pdf

Chronic Pain Therapeutics
Chronic pain patients - diminished descending pain 
inhibition 
Current first line treatments for chronic pain engage 
descending pain inhibitory system:
• Amine antidepressants: 
•
TCAs (tricyclic antidepressants):
•
Amitriptyline - serotonin norepinephrine 
reuptake inhibitor (SSNRI); 
•
Desipramine: norepinephrine-selective 
reuptake inhibitor (NSRI), weaker therapeutic 
effect
•
Floxetine: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor 
(SSRI), high dose can treat fibromyoalgia
•
Duloxetine: SNRI
The efficiency of conditioned pain 
modulation (CPM, “pain inhibits pain”) 
predicts the efficacy of duloxetine. 
Yarnitsky, D., et al. (2012), Pain 153(6): 1193-1198
