Osteopathic Approach to Diagnosis and Treatment 4e: Ch 59, Motion Testing, Innominate Rocking
• Ask your patient to lie supine
• Place your thenar and hypothenar eminences 
along the patients ASISs bilaterally
• Introduce gentle but firm posterior and medial 
pressure toward the ipsilateral sacro-iliac joint 
on one side at a time
– Slow
– Allow the ilia to recoil anteriorly against gentle 
pressure
• Assess for quality of motion
– One side usually offers more resistance
ASIS Compression Test (Pelvic Rock Test)

=== PAGE 17 ===
ASIS:
• Pt supine, physician standing with dominant eye 
closest to patient
• Stand so that your dominant eye can be over the 
vertical midline of the pt
• Facing patient’s head
• May need to lean your side over the table to get your eye 
midline
• Lay the edge of your thumbs perpendicular to the 
inferior aspect of each ASIS – snugly – to determine 
which ASIS is relatively superior and which is inferior
• Then, using the umbilicus as a landmark, determine 
the relationship of the ASISs to the midline. (Which 
ASIS is closer, which is farther from midline?)
Osteopathic Approach to Diagnosis and Treatment 4e: Ch 59, Static Examination
Pelvic Landmarks: Anterior
Relationship of anterior superior iliac spines to other pelvic structures.

=== PAGE 18 ===
Pubic tubercles:
• Same beginning steps as above
• **Let your patient know that you intend to examine the 
lower part of their pelvis at the pubic bone (consider 
showing the patient where this landmark is to be clear).
• Starting at the level of the umbilicus, gently inch down 
with the heel of your hand (fingers pointing cephalad) 
until it meets the bony pubic symphysis
• Then gently place your thumb (or index finger) pads 
over the superior aspect of the pubic tubercles bilaterally 
to determine which side is relatively superior and which 
is inferior
Osteopathic Approach to Diagnosis and Treatment 4e: Ch 59, Static Examination
Pelvic Landmarks: Anterior
Location of pubic tubercles.

=== PAGE 19 ===
Medial Malleoli:
• If not done already, have the patient “reseat” their 
pelvis by bending knees, feet flat on the table, lifting 
pelvis in the air and putting back pelvis then legs 
back down
• Gently add traction inferiorly at both ankles to take 
out any slack
• Place your thumb pads just inferior to the medial 
malleoli bilaterally to determine which side is 
relatively superior and which is inferior
Osteopathic Approach to Diagnosis and Treatment 4e: Ch 59, Static Examination

=== PAGE 39 ===
Pubic Compression Dysfunction 
(AKA ADducted pubic symphysis) -
Also called pubic adduction. A somatic dysfunction in which the pubic bones are 
forced toward each other at the pubic symphysis. This dysfunction is characterized 
by tenderness to palpation over the pubic symphysis, lack of apparent asymmetry, 
but associated with restricted motion of the pelvic ring (from FOM4 Glossary)
**Standing flexion test may be falsely neg or 
may lateralize to one side. It is considered as
indicative of innominate (which pubes are part
of) dysfunction.
**ASIS compression test may be falsely neg or 
may lateralize to one side; please keep in mind 
this test may indicate innominate OR sacral 
Dysfunction, but is useful as a screening test.
Foundations 3e, Ch. 41
Atlas of Osteopathic Tech 3e, Ch 10

=== PAGE 40 ===
• Pubic symphysis compression (also called pubic adduction) occurs when 
the pubic bones are forced toward each other at the symphysis.
• Suspicion of dysfunction by history (trauma, pregnancy, delivery)
• Standing flexion test may be positive on one or both sides (false negative)
• There is no apparent asymmetry of the pubic bones(The ASIS, PSIS 
heights appear equal). 
•
There is bilateral tenderness to palpation at the symphysis and often on 
each pubic rami as well.
• There is an associated restriction of motion of the pelvic ring (ASIS 
compression restricted B/L).
• Palpable bulging of the symphyseal cartilage
• May have urinary tract symptoms
Pubis Compression Dysfunction
FOM4e Ch41b and Atlas of Osteopathic Technique 4e, Ch10

=== PAGE 41 ===
Pubic Gapping Dysfunction 
(AKA ABducted pubic symphysis) -
Also called pubic abduction. A somatic dysfunction in which the pubic bones are 
pulled away from each other at the pubic symphysis. This dysfunction is frequently 
seen in women following childbirth (from FOM4 Glossary).
**Standing flexion test may be falsely neg or 
may lateralize to one side. It is considered as
indicative of innominate (which pubes are part
of) dysfunction.
**ASIS compression test may be falsely neg or 
may lateralize to one side; please keep in mind 
this test may indicate innominate OR sacral 
Dysfunction, but is useful as a screening test.
Foundations 3e, Ch. 41
Atlas of Osteopathic Tech 3e, Ch 10

Pubic symphysis gapping (also called pubic abduction) occurs when the pubic bones 
are forced away from each other at the symphysis.
•
Suspicion of dysfunction by history (trauma, pregnancy, delivery)
•
Standing flexion test may be positive on one or both sides (false negative)
•
There is no apparent asymmetry of the pubic bones (The ASIS, PSIS heights appear

•
Sulcus deeper than normal at the pubic symphysis
•

•

•
Gapping or subluxation of the pubic symphysis can occur after childbirth, after pelvic 
fractures, or other traumas. Pelvic x-rays clearly document the degree of gapping. 
These are usually quite painful, and not easily treated.

A 20-year-old college equestrian team member with a history of 
depression presents to your office complaining right lower back 
and hip pain since she fell from her horse during practice one 
month ago.  She says her right foot got caught in the stirrup when 
she fell off.  She was evaluated in the emergency the department 
the day of the injury.  Fortunately, she did not break any bones or 
sustain any other injuries.  The pain is located in the right buttock, 
pubic and inguinal area as well as in her lumbar spine are 
bilaterally.  The pain is 8/10 in severity and it is worse when she 
tries to ride her horse.  Consequently, she has not been able to 
ride for the past month and has been feeling very “down” lately.  
She denies any numbness, tingling or weakness in her legs.

Motion Testing and Static Landmarks exam to name somatic 
dysfunctions of the pelvis

flexion test
