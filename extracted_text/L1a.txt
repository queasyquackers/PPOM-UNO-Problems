=== PAGE 2 ===
Session Objectives
1. Describe the basic anatomy of the pelvis and explain how it relates to the 
biomechanics of this region.
2. Describe and discuss the standing flexion test, ASIS compression test, and 
the seated flexion test, including how to perform them, what a positive 
finding is in each, and what this means osteopathically for diagnosing 
innominate and pubic somatic dysfunction.
3. Compare and contrast the standing flexion test and seated flexion tests.
4. Identify the landmarks that are used to perform to perform the innominate 
and pubic bone screening tests
You will learn about diagnosing the various specific pelvic (ie. innominate and pubic) somatic dysfunctions in the pelvis lab.

What is somatic dysfunction? And what does that mean for pelvic 
biomechanics?
➢Impaired or altered function of related components of the body 
framework system: skeletal, arthrodial, and myofascial structures, 
and their related vascular, lymphatic, and neural elements. It is 
characterized by positional asymmetry, restricted range of motion, 
tissue texture abnormalities, and/or tenderness.
Somatic Dysfunction

=== PAGE 31 ===
• Ask your patient to lie supine
• Place your thenar and hypothenar eminences along the 
patients ASISs bilaterally
• Introduce gentle but firm posterior and medial pressure 
toward the ipsilateral sacro-iliac joint on one side at a time
– Slow
– Allow the ilia to recoil anteriorly against gentle pressure
• Assess for quality of motion
– One side usually offers more resistance
– Named for side that offers more resistance
ASIS Compression Test a.k.a. Pelvic Rock Test

=== PAGE 35 ===
Standing flexion test
• Patient standing
• Thumbs under the PSIS
• Look for side of PSIS that 
moves first and farthest
• Assesses iliosacral (ie. 
pelvic) SD
Seated flexion test
• Patient seated
• Thumbs under the PSIS
• Look for side of PSIS that 
moves first and farthest
• Assesses sacroiliac (ie. 
sacral) SD
Standing flexion test vs Seated flexion test

=== PAGE 37 ===
1.
Clearly identify the PSIS. Contacting the skin with thumbs would be 
ideal when you first learn this test (ask partner for permission)
2.
Keep your eyes level with the PSIS; keep dominant eye at midline of 
body
3.
Keep thumbs horizontal to the ground and inferior to each PSIS, and 
contact the same part of the PSIS with the same part of each thumb. 
This is key step #1.
4.
When patient bends forward, keep your thumbs under the PSIS instead 
of letting them slide over the PSIS. This is key step #2.
5.
Repeat your tests to ensure reliability whenever possible. This is key 
step #3.
6.
Perform the ASIS compression test if the test if the above are equivocal 
or negative.
Tips for success for the standing and seated 
flexion tests

The following slides are to introduce the various landmarks that 
are used to diagnose pelvic somatic dysfunction. In the lab prep 
lecture and OMM lab for the pelvis, you will learn how to 
combine the tests covered in this lecture with asymmetries in 
key landmark findings in order to diagnose the various pelvic (ie. 
innominate and pubic) somatic dysfunctions. The screening 
tests in this lecture as just part of the process to screen for 
pelvic SD. They are not sufficient for you to definitively 
diagnose pelvic SD. You need to examine the landmarks too!
