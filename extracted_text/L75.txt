--- SLIDE 8 ---
• Criteria: (simple)
-A convulsion associated with an elevated temperature 
greater than 38°C (100.4°F) (rate of rise)
-6 month to 5 years of age
-Lasts less than 15 minutes
-Tonic/Clonic
-Post-ictal phase after
-No prior neurologic issue
 
-No central nervous system infection or inflammation
 
-No acute systemic metabolic abnormality that may produce 
convulsions
 
-No history of previous afebrile seizures
Febrile Seizures

• Less than 15 min
• Age range 6months-5yrs
• One seizure in a 24hr 
period

ictal phase
• Usually reassurance 
and no further workup
Complex 
• Focal features
• Longer than 15 minutes
• Outside age range
• More than one episode in

• Residual symptoms or

phase
• Pre-existing neurological 
issues

Simple Vs Complex Febrile Seizure

--- SLIDE 11 ---
•
Etiology of febrile seizures is unclear
•
Recurrence rate varies: 30-65%, the more recurrences, the 
more likely to have more episodes
•
Genetic susceptibility: among first-degree relatives of children with 
febrile seizures, 10 to 20 percent of parents and siblings also have 
had or will have febrile seizures
•
Treatment: recommended if the febrile seizure lasts longer than 5 
minutes; options include lorazepam IV, fosphenytoin IV, rectal 
diazepam gel 
•
Neurologic sequelae are rare
•
Subsequent epilepsy frequency only slightly above general 
population

Infantile Spasms continued..

--- SLIDE 17 ---
• Also known as benign non-epileptic infantile spasms 
• Myoclonus, spasms with brief tonic contractions or 
shuddering
• First occurring at three to eight months of age, these 
clusters increase in intensity and severity over weeks or 
months and then remit spontaneously at two to three 
years of age 
• Typically occur when awake
• Spasms can occur in clusters
• Normal EEG, normal neurologic development without 
subsequent epilepsy
• Reassurance (after confident it’s not infantile spasms or 
other seizures)
Benign myoclonus of infancy

--- SLIDE 18 ---
•
Sudden, profound impairment of consciousness without loss 
of body tone characteristically provoked by hyperventilation 
•
May be subtle and often go unnoticed for prolonged periods, 
even with multiple seizures per day (often confused with 
inattention/daydreaming)
•
Median age of onset: 6 years old (Range 4-10)
•
Often remits by early puberty in most patients without major 
sequelae
•
Up to 20 percent of children have a history of febrile seizures 
and nearly half have a first or second degree relative with 
seizures
Absence Seizures

--- SLIDE 19 ---
• Requires all of the following:
-Age at onset of 4 to 10 years
-Normal neurological and developmental state
-Brief (4 to 20 seconds) and frequent (tens per 
day) absence seizures, with abrupt and 
loss/ alterations of consciousness
-Generalized rhythmic spike and wave 
discharges at around 3 Hz (EEG)
Need High index of suspicion!
Absence Sz –Diagnosis

--- SLIDE 22 ---
• Most common seizure type is a simple partial 
seizure with motor symptoms involving the face
• Age of onset peaks between 7-9 (Range 3-13)
• ¾ of seizures occur at night (falling asleep, 
asleep, or upon awakening)
• Electroencephalogram (EEG) findings of BECTS

distinctive features 
Benign partial epilepsy with central temporal spikes 
(BECTS) 
AKA Rolandic Epilepsy

--- SLIDE 23 ---
• Excellent prognosis, with spontaneous 
remission in the majority of patients 
before age 12 to 13 years
• Treatment: Antiepileptic drug (AED) 
treatment is often not recommended, 
particularly if only simple partial seizures 
occur and if the child and family are 
comfortable with this approach
BECTS continued..

--- SLIDE 24 ---
• Underlying cause unknown
• Typically occurs in otherwise healthy 
adolescents
• Triad: Myoclonic jerks, generalized tonic-
clonic seizures and absence seizures
• Photosensitivity is common
• Seizures occur upon awakening or in 
association with sleep deprivation
Juvenile Myoclonic epilepsy

--- SLIDE 25 ---
• Most patients with JME have normal 
global cognitive capacities
• Diagnosis: Interictal EEG pattern shows 
4-6 Hz bilateral polyspikes and slow wave 
discharges with frontal predominance 
over a normal background activity
Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy continued..

--- SLIDE 26 ---
• Treatment: generally respond quickly and 
completely to broad spectrum antiepileptic 
drug (AED) therapy, but most require 
lifelong treatment
• Valproic acid is first line (controls all 3 
seizure types), Lamotrigine
• Lifestyle modifications- sleep, hydration, 
avoid flickering lights
Juvenile Myoclonic epilepsy continued..

--- SLIDE 27 ---
•
Associated with severe seizures in childhood
•
Many causes: Tuberous sclerosis, meningitis, head injuries
•
Usually present in the first 7 years of life
•
Up to 25% have a history of infantile spasms
•
Clinical features characterized by:
-Multiple seizure types
-A slow (less than 2.5 Hertz) spike-wave pattern on the interictal 
EEG that is generalized and of highest voltage in the frontal 
region (also called an "atypical spike and wave" pattern)
-Cognitive dysfunction (occasionally progressive), psychotic 
symptoms
Lennox-Gestaut Syndrome 

--- SLIDE 28 ---
• Hard to control usually need more than 
one AED 
• Treatment- Valproate, Topiramate, 
Lamotrigine, 
• The optimal therapy for LGS is uncertain, 
no drug has been shown to be highly 
effective
• Ketogenic Diet
