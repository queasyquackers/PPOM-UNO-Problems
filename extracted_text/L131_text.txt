Basic concepts
Rheum disease = History + Physical exam findings + autoimmune 
markers + serologic tests + pathology + imaging.
What clues you in that your patient may have a Rheumatologic 
condition?
Fevers
Arthralgias (joint pain) WITH arthritis (inflammation of the joint) 
Timing (Morning stiffness – gets better as you use it more)
Rash
Weakness/Fatigue
Duration of symptoms
What you don't want to miss
Cancer (ALL/neuroblastoma)
Infection (viral illness, septic arthritis, lyme, osteomyelitis)
Mimics: metabolic (thyroid), orthopedic (patellofemoral syndrome, 
growing pains, chronic pain conditions (fibromyalgia, depression), other 
autoimmune disorders (IBD), vasculitidis

Basic concepts
For diagnosis: complete Physical Exam (as disease is multisystem)
Thorough Skin exam + scalp (generalized skin rash, sparing nasal labial 
folds, involving ears/around eyes, lower extremities, hair patterns)
Nail Beds (dilated capillary loops) - Nailfold capillaroscopy
Oropharynx: ulcers (hard palate involved in SLE), mucositis, TMJ joint 
instability/inflammation
Lymph nodes: lymphadenopathy
Eyes: uveitis, conjunctivitis
Heart: pericardial rubs 
Lungs: pleural effusions, ILD
All Joints: Arthritis defined as intra-articular swelling of 2 or more of the 
following findings (pain on motion, range of motion limitation and 
warmth), limb length discrepancy
Pictures from NEJM, Ulcer picture from AAFP

Basic labs:
Lymphopenia more specific to SLE than Leukopenia, in sJIA WBC Elevated
PLTs elevated – due to inflammation
Hbg low – anemia of chronic disease – nonspecific
ESR, CRP elevation– non specific
Rheumatoid Factor + is present in <10% of children with JIA – poor 
sensitivity. Co-relate with symptoms = strong prognostic indicator
Anti- cyclic citrullinated protein (CCP) = RF pos JIA pretty specific
Anti- nuclear antibody – low specificity – not recommended for screening 
but correlate with symptoms
DsDNA = SLE
Anti-Smith = SLE
SSA (Ro) positive = SLE, sjrogren syndrome
SSA (La) positive = SLE, sjrogren syndrome
Anti-histone = Drug induced lupus
Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs) = vasculitis
Anti- topoisomerase (Scl-70) = systemic sclerosis – rare in childhood
Muscle Enzymes – ALT, AST, CK, Aldolase, LDH
Urine – protein, can do protein/creatitine ratio
Imaging: Plain xrays
MRI more sensitive for early 
erosive arthritis
Echo
PFTs

Common Pathologies 
JIA (Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis) 
Most common rheumatic disease in children, clinical diagnosis
Age at onset: < 16 years old 
Arthritis: > or = 1 joint
Duration: > or = 6 weeks
Type of articular involvement - many subtypes :
Poly: > or = 5 joints
RF neg or RF pos
Oligo: < or = 4 joints
Systemic-onset disease: arthritis with rash and quotidian fever
Psoriatic arthritis
Enthesitis-related arthritis 

=== PAGE 10 ===
Epidemiology
JIA: wide ranging numbers depending on environmental exposure, 
immunogenic susceptibility, variations in diagnostic criteria, lack of 
population based data.
Estimated 300,000 children in US have arthritis
Oligoarthritis being the most common, peak age at onset 2-4 years of 
age.  Polyarthritis is bimodal (2-4 yrs,10-14yrs), sJIA (1-5 yrs)
No sex predominance for systemic JIA. Otherwise most other 
subtypes: girls > boys, except Enthesitis which is more common in 
boys.
Etiology: 
Complex genetic trait – affecting both humeral and cellular immunity 
– MCH I and II, protein tyrosine phosphatase non receptor 22 
(PTPN22), TNF- alpha, IL-6, IL-1alpha. Double hit - usually with 
external factor. T cells recruited for non-self synovial antigens, cause 
inflammation cascade, including adding B cells and complement

Joints affected:
Oligo: larger joints
Polyarthritis: both upper, lower joints. Can also affect TMJs, 
cervical  spine involvement and hip disease
Enthesitis – lower limb joints
Treatment:
NSAIDs (Naproxen, Ibuprofen, Meloxicam) 
DMARDs (Methotrexate PO or SC weekly)
Biologics (Anti-TNF-alpha, anti-IL1 receptor)
Intra-articular corticosteroids
Systemic steroids – generally avoided unless bridging or severe 
acute disease

=== PAGE 13 ===
Prognosis:
Goal is disease remission
40-60% of patients get better with 6 weeks of NSAIDs
Oligo fares better than Poly
Depends on disease severity, earlier age at 
presentation, RF seropositive, disease involving hip, 
hand, wrist generally have poorer prognosis
Complications include leg discrepancy, micrognathia, 
contractures, persistent uncontrolled uveitis can cause 
chronic scarring and blindness

Enthesitis related arthritis (ERA)
Classification Criteria
1. Arthritis PLUS Enthesitis
2. Arthritis OR Enthesitis with 2 of more of the following
o
Sacroiliac joint tenderness or inflammatory 
lumbosacral pain
o
Presence of HLA-B27
o
Onset of arthritis in a male older than 6 years
o
Acute anterior Uveitis
o
Family history of HLA-B27 associated disease in 1st 
degree relative
3. Typically first 6 months of disease are asymmetric and have 
fewer than 4 joints affected (inflammation of small joints in 
foot – highly suggestive of ERA)

=== PAGE 17 ===
Juvenile Ankylosing Spondylitis (JAS)
Frequently begins with oligoarthritis and enthesitis
Classification Criteria  (modified New York criteria)
1. Arthritis PLUS Enthesitis
2. Arthritis OR Enthesitis with 2 of more of the following
o
Sacroiliac joint tenderness or inflammatory lumbosacral pain
o
Presence of HLA-B27 ( 90% of children with JAS)
o
Onset of arthritis in a male older than 6 years
o
Acute anterior Uveitis
o
Family history of HLA-B27 associated disease in 1st degree relative
3. Typically first 6 months of disease are asymmetric and have fewer than 4 
joints affected 
4. Treatment: goals of therapy to control inflammation, prevent ankylosis 
(fusion of bones) with DMARDS, NSAIDS, Physical Therapy 
5. Prognosis: if ongoing disease activity for >5 yrs = disability very likely , 
remission in < 20% of those patients

=== PAGE 18 ===
Psoriatic Arthritis
Classification Criteria
1. Arthritis PLUS Psoriasis
2. Arthritis OR Psoriasis  with 2 of more of the following
o
Dactylitis
o
Nail pitting or onycholysis
o
Family history of Psoriasis in 1st degree relative
3. Disease onset peaks during preschool and early adolescent years

Reactive Arthritis
Quick review
Defined as joint inflammation caused by a sterile inflammatory reaction after 
recent infection 3d to 6wks
Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree – not common in kids. Usually more 
systemic symptoms (fatigue, weakness), cutaneous manifestations (erythematous 
oral macules, plaques, erosions, erythema nodosum, fingertip erosions and more 
enthesitis and dactylitis)
Arthritis is asymmetric, oligoarticular of lower extremities.
Bacteria: Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Campylobacter, GU tract infections with 
Chlamydia, Ecoli and C diff less common
Virus: Adeno, CMV, EBV, HSV, Parvovirus, Rubella
75% of pts with reactive arthritis are HLA-B27 positive. Tx NSAIDS
If Chlamydia ongoing = TREAT the bug

Pediatric Onset SLE (pSLE)
Compared with adults, kids have more severe disease and widespread 
organ involvement
In adolescents: renal failure or nephrotic syndrome – 1st presenting sign
Prevalence of SLE in children 1-6/100,000 highest among African, Asian, 
Hispanic, Native American descent. Childhood SLE is rare before 5yr, 
median age at diagnosis 11-12 years. 20% of SLE pts are diagnosed before 
age 16yr.
Most common presenting sign in pSLE fever, fatigue, hematologic 
abnormalities, arthralgias and arthritis (symmetric polyarthritis large and 
small joints). Neuropsych complications hard on peds patients
Antiphospholipid antibodies found in 66% of pSLE
Tx: hydroxychloroquine, avoid sun exposure, steroids, MTX, biologics
Prognosis: 5yr and 10 yr survival with $$ countries 99, 97%, $ countries 85, 
79%

=== PAGE 22 ===
Neonatal Lupus (NLE)
Not autoimmune disease but immunoglobulins Anti-Ro, Anti-La crossing 
over into fetal circulation
Moms are mostly asymptomatic, 50% have defined SLE or Sjogrens
Resulting in malar rash, congenital heart block (ranging from type 1 to type 3), 
cytopenias, hepatitis
It is thought there are Ro and La antigens on cardiac cells specially the AV 
node which then cause the fibrosis and varying degrees of block
Complete heart block is permanent – needs pacing, occasional IVIG, steroids 
used. Cardiomyopathy also a bad prognosis – need transplant
Data mixed but using steroids, IVIG to lower Anti-Ro and La antibiotics seem 
to help prevent occurrence in future child (15-20%)
Prenatally, can detect heart abnormalities via Echo, helpful to see a ped 
cardiologist
Usual Treatment: time, avoidance of sun

=== PAGE 23 ===
Juvenile Dermatomyositis (JDM)
Characterized by proximal muscle weakness and distinctive rashes of face and 
extensor joint surfaces
3 cases in 1 million children in 1 year. Peak age onset 7 yrs. F:M 2:1
Genetics in HLA genes and TNF alpha, IL-1 receptor mutations with environmental 
factors
Children with JDM present with either rash, insidious onset of weakness or BOTH
Fevers, dysphagia, arthritis, muscle tenderness and fatigue are also common
UV light exposure with generalized erythema
Shawl sign: If over chest/neck
Heliotrope rash: blue-violet discoloration of eyelids, erythema crosses nasolabial 
folds
Gottron papules: bright pink, pale, shiny, thickened atrophic plaques over hand 
joints. Rash can be mistaken for eczema or psoriasis
Mechanic's hands: thickened erythematous scaly rash over palms and soles

=== PAGE 24 ===
JDM 
Weakness is symmetric, muscles such as neck flexors, 
shoulder girdle, hip flexors. Trouble combing hair, climbing 
stairs, getting out of bed
Esophageal and resp muscles can also be affected. Swallow 
test maybe helpful. Respiratory failure – hypercarbia with 
hypoxia, no iWOB, often need NIF 
EMG shows signs of myopathy. Labs show muscle 
inflammation, muscle biopsy shows necrosis, serologic 
testing, ANA is pos in >80% of patients, RF is neg, Anemia, ESR 
nml. Xrays can show calcifications
Tx: MTX, steroids, IVIG (Ped Rheum is managing), PT/OT, aovid 
sun 
Prognosis: Mortality 1% but little known about long term 
consequences, respond well to therapy

Behcet disease (BD)
Spectrum of symptoms is broad - vasculitis of arteries + veins 
Autoinflammatory nature: polygenic disorder, innate immunity activation 
Prevalence in children is 10% of adult prevalence  +FH in 20% of cases. 
Mean age at first symptom 8-10 years of age. 
Starts with painful oral ulcer does not scar, genital ulcers heal with scars. 
Anterior Uveitis. Skin lesions like erythema nodosum 
Meet 3 of the criteria in chart 
Tx: Azathioprine for eye disease. Topical tx for ulcers, colchicine, 
sucralfate, steroids. Mortality in children with BD low except if they get 
pulm aneurysm

Juvenile Sjogren's Disease
Rare in children, classic signs of dry eyes and dry mouth
Mean age at diagnosis 9-10 yr. 75% are F
Diagnostic criteria in children proposed but not validated
Recurrent parotid gland enlargement and parotiditis common in children ( 
>70%). Children higher rate of systemic sx, fever, adenopathy compared to 
adults. In kids also dental caries as a result of dry mouth.
Diagnosis based on clinical features, supported by biopsy of salivary or 
parotid gland -> lymphocytic infiltration (children more likely to have nml 
salivary and abnormal parotid biopsies) need 4 of 12 items
Elevated ESR, positive RF, hypergammaglobulinemia, presence of SSA and 
SSB antibodies
Treatment: Artificial tears, massage parotids, oral lozenges, fluids. 
Steroids, NSAIDS, hydroxychloroquine

=== PAGE 27 ===
Scleroderma (systemic and localized)
Annual incidence rare in children 1-3 per 100,000 children
Mean age onset 7-9 years. More common in Females
Most cases of localized scleroderma, onset starts in 
childhood. Systemic only 10%
Systemic scleroderma: pulmonary fibrosis, myocardial 
fibrosis, myopathy, joint pain, more favorable in children than 
adults
Localized: usually limited to skin and subcutaneous tissue
Systemic sclerosis. Patients with calcinosis cutis, Raynaud 
phenomenon, esophageal dysfunction, sclerodactyly, and 
telangiectasia (CREST) syndrome

=== PAGE 28 ===
Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF)
Autosomal recessive pathogenic variant in MEFV (amino acid protein 
denoted pyrin (greek for fever), found in granulocytes, monocytes and 
in fibroblasts. Causing anto-inflammatory disease.
Occurs primarily among Mediterranean ancestry (Jewish, Turkish, 
Armenian, Arab, Italian)
Short Recurrent (1-3d) fever, serositis, mono or pauciarticular arthritis 
or rash. Most present with sx in childhood, 90% present before 20 
years. So fever + abd pain or chest pain, arthritis and rash = sounds 
familiar.
Distinguishing factors: in between fever episodes, no symptoms and 
cutaneous finding erysipeloid erythematous rash on ankle.
Episodes triggered by stress, infections or menses. Inflammatory 
markers will be elevated.     
 Frequency of attacks vary from weekly to one to two flares per year
Tx: prophylactic daily oral colchicine decreases frequency, severity 
intensity of flares
