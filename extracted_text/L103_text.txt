Normal Muscle Structure
• Sarcomere (z line to z line)
– I band (actin filament alone)
– A band (myosin and actin filament overlap area)
– H band (myosin filament alone)
– M line (attachment point of thick filaments)
• Types of Myofibers
– Type 1: example, soleus/paraspinal muscles
•
Slow, red fibers with high levels of mitochondria
•
Sustained contractions through oxidative phosphorylation
•
Utilized in endurance training
– Type 2: example, triceps muscle
•
Fast, white fibers with low levels of mitochondria
•
Quickly fatigue due to anaerobic glycolysis
•
Utilized in weight/resistance training or sprinting

Muscle Contraction
(1) Action potential opens voltage-
gated calcium channels on the 
presynaptic membrane
(2) Acetylcholine (ACh) is released 
and bind to the ACh receptors on 
the postsynaptic membrane 
causing
(3) Motor end plate depolarization,
T-tubules connect neighboring 
muscle cells to coordinate 
contraction
(4) Depolarization opens the 
dihydropyridine and ryanodine 
receptors that allow for calcium 
release from the sarcoplasmic 
reticulum

Myogenic Injury
•
Segmental Myofiber Degeneration and Regeneration
– Occurs when part of a myofiber undergoes necrosis
– Degeneration
• Creatine kinase released into blood from damaged cells
• Myophagocytosis occurs (macrophages remove damaged components of 
the myofiber)
– Regeneration
• Begins with fusion of an activated satellite cell to the damaged myofiber
• Regenerated myofibers stain basophilic due to high levels of RNA
• In chronic disease, complete regeneration is not possible due to the 
disease process outpacing regeneration
•
Myofiber hypertrophy is typically a physiologic response to exercise but can be 
associated with a disease processes
•
Cytoplasmic inclusions include vacuoles, protein aggregates, clustered organelles
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

Neurogenic Injuries
•
Disruption of innervation of a 
muscle that causes muscle 
dysfunction (usually occurs as 
grouped atrophy, E)
•
Mechanism: innervating motor 
neuron determines fiber type
–
Denervation causes atrophy of 
those fibers as a group 
(flattened, angular shape)
–
Reinnervation will normalize the 
shape but may change the fiber 
type; axons belonging to 
unaffected nearby motor unit 
extend sprouts to reinnervate 
myocytes; newly reinnervated 
fibers become fiber type of that 
motor unit: loss of 
checkerboard pattern, D 
(histologically motor units 
appear as groups of myofibers 
of the same histochemical type 
(fiber type grouping, F))
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

Neurogenic vs myopathic injury
•
Neurogenic atrophy of muscle
–
Caused by disorders that affect motor 
neurons
–
Motor neuron diseases are progressive, 
destroy anterior horn cells in spinal cord 
and cranial nerve motor neurons
–
Denervation causes atrophy of those 
fibers as a group (flattened, angular 
shape)
•
Myopathic disorder
–
Single fiber necrosis, and regeneration 
–
Necrotic cells lose stain of nuclei with 
pale cytoplasm, shrink, irregular cell 
membrane, also cytoplasm irregular 
stain with condensed areas
–
Increased variability in fiber size, 
including hypertrophy and/or atrophy 
–
Regenerating myofiber show increased 
central nucleation, more basophilic 
initially on H&E then enlarge, 
hyperchromatic cytoplasm, then nuclei 
peripheral cytoplasm less 
hyperchromatic

Spinal Muscular Atrophy: SMA I 
•
Disorders with childhood onset, progressive weakness
– SMA I = Werdnig-Hoffman’s Disease; most severe
•
Pathogenesis: Autosomal Recessive: loss of fxn mutation in SMN1 gene (chrom 5) 
[possible role in RNA splicing (snRNP assembly)] – causes defects in spliceosomes
– Loss of motor neuron innervation (anterior horn cell 
destruction/degeneration) → muscle weakness and atrophy
– Degeneration of the anterior horns (motor neurons) of the spinal cord
– Denervation of skeletal muscle
•
Clinical features –LMN signs
– Infants: can present with generalized hypotonia (“floppy infant”)-little muscle 
tone, presents within 4 months of birth; death usually by 2 - 3 years
•
Diagnostic features - biopsy
– Histology: large areas of flattened, angulated, very atrophic myofibers
mixed with normal/hypertrophied myofibers 
in small groups (those that retain innervation)

Muscular Dystrophies
• Progressive muscle weakness and wasting, 
usually beginning in early childhood 
• Two most common: Duchenne  MD and Becker 
MD
• X-linked – gene located in Xp21 region, encodes 
dystrophin
• Duchenne - more severe, usually leads to death 
by 20s – 30s.
• Becker – milder phenotype, later onset of disease

DMD Histopathology
•
Early disease:
– muscle biopsies in young boys: ongoing muscle 
damage, i.e., segmental myofiber 
degeneration and regeneration associated 
with admixture of atrophic myofibers
– myophagocytosis, usually no inflammation
– fascicular architecture still preserved
•
As DMD progresses: 
– muscle tissue replaced by collagen and fat 
cells (“fatty infiltration”, image) - causes 
pseudohypertrophy of calves
– remaining myofibers: prominent variation in 
size (small atrophic fibers to large hypertrophied 
fibers)
– fascicular architecture distorted, abnormal

• Duchenne MD manifested by age 5 
years  - Walking may be delayed 
• Life expectancy 25-30 years
• Weakness in pelvic girdle muscles, 
extends to shoulder muscles
• Enlargement of calf muscles associated

=== PAGE 19 ===
DMD
•
Clinical features: Usually Proximal muscle weakness and clumsiness
– Pseudohypertrophy of the calf muscles: enlargement of the 
muscle with associated weakness (muscles are replaced by 
collagen and fat tissue)
– Gower’s Sign: use of their hands to stand from a lying/sitting position
– Later, develop joint contractures, scoliosis, worsening respiratory 
reserve and sleep hypoventilation
– Can develop cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias (protein is located 
primarily in skeletal muscles and in heart, small amounts of dystrophin 
are present in nerve cells in brain)
– May have intellectual impairment
– Usually increased serum creatine kinase (early stages)
– Death may result from respiratory insufficiency by the 20s – 30s
•
Treatment: supportive; New efforts to manipulate RNA splicing, promotion of 
ribosomal “read-through” of stop codons
First Aid, 2021
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

mutation of the dystrophin gene

• Clinical features:
– Similar to Duchenne but less severe and slower progression
– Presents later
– May have normal life expectancy

Myotonic Dystrophy
•
Pathogenesis: Autosomal dominant, CTG trinucleotide repeat
•
Age of onset 20s-40 years
•
Length of expansion correlates with disease severity (similar to Huntington 
disease)
•
Triplet expansion disrupts normal splicing 
– Repeat is expanded in the DMPK gene that encodes for myotonin 
protein kinase
– Disruption of RNA splicing causes deficiency of CLC1 (chloride 
channel) normally involved with muscle relaxation: leading to 
myotonia

Ion Channel Myopathies
• These skeletal muscle channelopathies are 
inherited mutations affecting function of ion 
channel proteins
– Some increase excitability (hypertonia), some 
decreased excitability (hypotonia)
– Some are subclassified based on K levels (examples, 
hyperkalemic, or hypokalemic, or normokalemic 
periodic paralysis)
– Depending on affected ion channel, clinical 
manifestations may include: 
• epilepsy, migraines
• movement disorders with cerebellar dysfunction
• peripheral nerve disease, muscle disease
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 26 ===
Ion Channel Myopathies cont.
• Some examples of mutations assoc. with muscle 
dysfunction:
• CACNA1S protein is subunit of muscle calcium channel and 
a missense mutation most common cause of hypokalemic 
paralysis
• CLC1 mutation affects chloride channel, associated with 
myotonia congenita, and CLC1 expression decreased in 
myotonic dystrophy
• Mutations RYR1 → disrupt function of ryanodine 
receptor (regulates calcium release from the 
sarcoplasmic reticulum) and are involved in congenital 
myopathy (central core disease) and malignant 
hyperthermia 
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 27 ===
Malignant Hyperthermia
• Rare but life-threatening skeletal muscle channelopathies 
Pathogenesis: Autosomal dominant: Mutation in the 
voltage-gated RYR1 gene affects ryanodine-r (involved with 
controlling release of Ca 2+ from sarcoplasmic reticulum)
– Increased calcium release from sarcoplasmic reticulum 
following administration of halogenated inhaled 
anesthetics and succinylcholine (temporary paralysis, 
rapid onset and short duration of muscle relaxation)
– Mechanism: Interaction of anesthetic and mutated 
receptor causes incr. calcium efflux which leads to 
tetany and heat production
First Aid, 2021
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27
27

• Hypermetabolic state
– Hyperthermia and later, hyperpyrexia (temp > 106 degrees 
F)

– Tachycardia/Tachypnea
• May occur in predisposed patients with hereditary muscle

Mitochondrial Myopathies
•
Rare disorders; may involve many organ systems
•
Pathogenesis: Involves mutations of mitochondrial DNA with maternal inheritance because 
only oocyte contributes mitochondria to embryo. Disease is thought to be due to mutations 
that impair ATP generation in mitochondria
–
All children of an affected female may show signs of disease due to maternal 
transmission (affected males can’t transmit to their children), has varying degrees of 
expressivity 
–
Tend to affect skeletal muscles/other tissues with high ATP requirements (cardiac 
muscle cells-dysrhythmia, neurons-seizures), drug therapy for MERRF syndrome 
includes metabolic drugs involved with or improve cellular respiration (ex. metformin, 
creatine, and L-carnitine), clonazepam for myoclonus and anticonvulsants
–
Skeletal muscle involvement causes weakness, increase in serum CK, or rhabdomyolysis 
–
Weakness in certain regions is variable, but involvement of extraocular eye muscles 
common
–
Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (inability to move eyes, eyelids and 
eyebrows – blurred, double vision) is common feature of mitochondrial disorders
First Aid, 2021
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 37 ===
Mitochondrial Myopathies 
•
Myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (MERRF)
–
Multisystem mitochondrial diseases characterized by predominantly 
intermittent muscle twitches aggravated by action (progressive 
myoclonus, hiccups are a form of myoclonus, also the sudden jerks, or 
"sleep starts," you may feel just before falling asleep - these forms of 
myoclonus occur in healthy people and rarely present a problem), 
weakness (myopathy – limb girdle type), and progressive stiffness 
(spasticity), diagnosis/biopsy: “ragged red fibers” due to accumulation 
of affected mitochondria in the subsarcolemmal area of myofibers, 
increased serum creatine kinase
–
Most also experience other types of seizures in addition to myoclonus, 
may be of generalized tonic-clonic, atonic or absence types
–
Modified Gomori trichrome stain allows visualization of the 
mitochondrial activity and collection by staining red (ragged red fibers), 
Pathogenic variant in mitochondrial gene MT-TK is present in more than 
80% of affected
•
Leber hereditary optic neuropathy
–
Predominantly degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their axons 
leads to an acute or subacute loss of central vision; it primarily affects 
young adult males; different mitochondrial mutation from MERRF
–
Commonly present with chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia 
(weakness of the extraocular eye muscles)
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed. 2021, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 39 ===
Dermatomyositis
•
Rare inflammation of skin and skeletal muscle; Immune-mediated small 
blood vessel injury leads to muscle damage, peak age onset 50-60 years, 
more common in females, gradually worsens over weeks to months, can 
last several years or be lifelong
– Classic skin rash: Lilac or heliotrope (reddish-purple rash on or around 
the eyelids) with periorbital edema
– May have Gottron's lesions/papules (scaling erythematous eruption or 
dusky red patches on knuckles, elbows, or knees).
• Proximal muscles weak first; symmetric, slow onset, myalgias
• Dysphagia may develop if oropharyngeal, esophageal muscles 
involved
– Extramuscular involvement includes interstitial lung disease (10%) 
vasculitis and myocarditis
– 15 - 24% of adults with dermatomyositis may have  malignancy (Lung, 
breast, ovary, colorectal, cervical, bladder, nasopharyngeal, esophageal, 
kidney, lymphatic, hematopoietic)
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th ed. 2014, Ch. 27

=== PAGE 40 ===
Dermatomyositis 
• Pathogenesis: Autoimmune disease characterized by small 
vessel damage, capillaries attacked by Abs and complement 
causing foci of ischemic myocyte necrosis
• Although the cause of dermatomyositis is unknown, several 
genetic, immunologic, and environmental factors are implicated, 
having another autoimmune disease is a risk factor 
• Anti-Mi2 Abs (anti-helicase) associated with Gottron papules 
and heliotropic rash
• Anti-Jo1 Abs (anti-histidyl t-RNA synthetase): associated with 
interstitial lung disease, non-erosive arthritis and 
“mechanic’s hand” rash
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 9th ed. 2014, Ch. 27

involvement, occurs mainly in adults, (peak age onset 30-60 years) and may have 
inflammatory involvement of heart, lungs, and blood vessels, women are affected more often 
than men, can last several years or be lifelong
•
Pathogenesis: Immune-mediated (CD8+ cytotoxic T cells) tissue damage, there is no 
vascular injury (in contrast with dermatomyositis)
•
Polymyositis is not caused by a genetic mutation, but some genes may influence the risk of 
developing polymyositis, having another autoimmune disease is a risk factor, exact cause 
unknown, but  has characteristics of autoimmune disorders, which occur when the immune 
system mistakenly attacks healthy body tissues
•
Histology: Endomysial inflammation (predominately CD8+ T cells) 
-
Necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers scattered throughout fascicle
-
No perifascicular atrophy (seen in dermatomyositis); no evidence of vascular damage
-
Clinical Findings: myalgia + symmetric proximal muscle weakness
-
Labs: increased serum creatine kinase
-
Treatment: immunosuppressive agents

Myasthenia Gravis 
•
Autoimmune - immune mediated loss of acetycholine 
receptors 
•
Pathogenesis: Auto-abs against post-synaptic 
acetylcholine receptors (~85%), against muscle 
acetylcholine receptor (AChR-MG) - leads to destruction of 
receptors and limited response of myofibers to Ach
•
Pathologic mechanisms
– complement fixation-mediated postsynaptic membrane 
damage
– postsynaptic membranes show morphologic alterations, 
depletion of Ach-r leads to limited myofiber response to 
Ach

•
Clinical features: 
– Bimodal distribution; young females (20-40 years), older males (50-70 years), 
more commonly affects women (2:1), if arising before age 40 years
– Painless weakness, fatigue—worsens with repeated exertion
– Weakness in proximal muscles, diaphragm, neck extension
– Diplopia, ptosis
– Dysphagia for both solids and liquids
– Reflexes spared
– Thymic abnormalities associated (thymoma, thymic hyperplasia)—esp in 
younger pts (10% of patients have a thymoma, 30% of patients (usually younger) 
have thymic hyperplasia)
– Electrophysiologic studies show decreased muscle response with repeated 
stimulation
– Nerve conduction studies normal
•
Treatment: Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are first line (inhibit 
acetylcholinesterase which increases acetylcholine and reverses muscle weakness)
– Others: plasmapheresis, immunosuppressive drugs, thymectomy (if thymoma)

Lambert Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome
•
Rare autoimmune disease, more common in males, onset age peaks 
at 35 years, with a larger peak at 60 years
•
Pathogenesis: autoantibodies against pre-synaptic calcium 
channels that prevents the release of Ach
•
Clinical features: Muscle weakness that improves with exertion
–
Autonomic symptoms (dry mouth, constipation, impotence)
–
Hyporeflexia
–
50% of patients have an associated malignancy 
(neuroendocrine lung carcinoma, e.g., small cell carcinoma)
–
Patients may have other autoimmune diseases as well
•
Diagnosis: electrophysiologic studies
–
Repetitive stimulation increases the muscle response (opposite 
of MG)
•
Treatment:
–
Drugs that increase the release of ACh into the synapse
–
Immunosuppressive agents

•

CLC1 deficiency, myotonia, muscle wasting, cataracts, frontal balding, testicular atrophy, etc.
