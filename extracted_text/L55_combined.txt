Session Objectives
1. Describe cerebrovascular pathology, including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic 
attack, and brain trauma.
2. Outline the histological changes in ischemic infarction based on the time since the ischemic event.
3. Explain the etiologies and classifications of ischemic strokes, including thrombotic, embolic, and global 
hypoperfusion.
4. Classify and differentiate hemorrhagic strokes:
1. Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH): Hypertension vs. Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA).
2. Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH): Traumatic, Aneurysmal, and Arteriovenous Malformations 
(AVMs).
5. Identify and describe the pathology of cerebrovascular trauma, including brain herniation, skull fractures, 
concussion, subdural, and epidural hematomas.
6. Discuss and describe the pathogenesis and morphological principles of direct parenchymal injuries, 
including contusion, coup-contrecoup injury, diffuse axonal injury.

Cerebrovascular disease (CVD)
• Common is US
• All disorders with an area of the brain temporarily or permanently affected by 
ischemia or bleeding and one or more cerebral blood vessels are involved in the 
pathological processes
• AKA Stroke = Brain blood flow suddenly reduced with cell hypoxia due to 
hypoxemia (low blood O2) or ischemia (low blood flow) causes cell disability and 
death/infarction:
• When blood to the brain is reduced, tissue survival depends on collateral circulation 
presence, ischemia duration (sustained → infarction) & magnitude and rapidity of 
reduction 
• Collateral flow source is circle of Willis, external carotid-ophthalmic artery 
collaterals - Little if any collateral flow for thalamus, basal ganglia & deep white 
matter deep penetrating vessels

Ischemic stroke
• Acute neurologicalconditionwith arterial supply is impaired - cause is occlusion 
of an upstream artery → possible infarction (pathological process results in 
necrotic tissue due to acute interruption of blood flow)
• Ischemia→ tissue hypoxia → irreversibleneuronal injury (can be as fast as 5 min
after hypoxia)
• Sustained ischemia → infarction → liquefactivenecrosis (digestion of dead cells 
by hydrolytic enzymes from inflammatory cells or microorganisms with complete 
loss of cellular architecture, results in liquid viscous mass)
• Over time, macrophages clear the debris, leading to the formation of a cystic 
space
• Grossly, little change in first 6 hours of irreversible injury: 48 hours, pale, soft, 
and swollen, gray-white matter junction indistinct; 2-10 days, brain becomes 
gelatinous and friable; 10 days-3 weeks, tissue liquefies, leaving fluid-filled 
(reabsorbed) cavity

Thrombotic ischemic stroke: Atherosclerotic 
cerebrovascular disease
• Most common type of stroke (~40%)
• Associated with hypertension and diabetes
• Caused by thrombus (blood clot that may fragment and embolize) 
developing in arteries supplying the brain that cause progressive 
luminal narrowing
• Seen in older persons, especially those with high cholesterol and 
atherosclerosis 
• Common sequence of events: Atherosclerotic plaque rupture → thrombus 
formation → ischemia → infarct
• Most common at carotid bifurcation, middle cerebral artery origin & 
either end of the basilar artery

Thrombotic ischemic stroke: Lipohyalinosis
• Pathological process affects small arteries (arteriosclerosis) and arterioles (arteriolosclerosis), 
associated with hypertension & diabetes, can lead to lacunar strokes
• Arteries thicken/ narrow due to fatty (lipo) and glassy/proteinaceous (hyaline) material: Ex. 
Lenticulostriate arteries accumulation of lipid and hyaline material have thickened arterial walls → 
narrowing and potential occlusion
• Chronic hypertensive vasculopathy → lipohyalinosis of the small vessels → occlusion of small, 
penetrating arteries → lacunar stroke (basal ganglia & subcortical white matter most prominent)
• Named for the characteristic “lake-like” appearance of the resulting cavities in the 
brain tissue on MRI of the brain
• Axial T2-weighted brain MRI showing hyperintense signals in the basal 
ganglia/internal capsule region and other deep brain structures (hyperintensity
causes include stroke, demyelination, inflammation, or tumors)
• Commonly affected regions: Internal capsule, basal ganglia, pons, corona radiata
• Depending on location, clinically silent or severe impairment

Hemorrhagic stroke
• Hemorrhagic stroke contributes to 10%-20% of strokes annually
• Hemorrhagic stroke is due to bleeding into the brain by the 
rupture of a blood vessel and is subdivided into intracerebral 
hemorrhage (ICH) & subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)
• ICH is bleeding directly into the brain tissue (parenchyma) and is 
commonly caused by hypertension or cerebral amyloid angiopathy 
(CAA)
• SAH is bleeding in the space around the brain – commonly caused 
by a ruptured aneurysm, head trauma, or an arteriovenous 
malformation (AVM)
• More common in men and increases with age
• In people on anticoagulant treatment, hemorrhagic infarcts may be 
extensive 
• Venous infarcts are often hemorrhagic - may occur after 
thrombotic occlusion of superior sagittal sinus, other sinuses, or 
deep cerebral veins
• Epidural hemorrhage (EDH) & subdural hemorrhages  (SDH) are 
types of intracranial bleeding & can cause stroke-like symptoms 
and rarely true ischemic stroke due to compression, but it's often 
misdiagnosed as stroke because it mimics symptoms

SAH: Spontaneous- (art eriovenous malformations ) AVM
• Important cause of spontaneous SAH, but can also 
result in intracerebral hemorrhage
• Region of dysplastic dilated blood vessels (neither 
arteries nor veins) arterial blood flows directly into 
draining veins without normal interposed capillary beds
• Usually congenital and enlarge with age, and often 
progress from low-flow juvenile lesions at birth to 
medium-to-high-flow high-pressure lesions in adulthood
• Although lacking features of typical neoplasms, AVMs 
frequently have activating mutations in KRAS oncogene in 
endothelial cells
• Grossly are “tangle of vessels” in subarachnoid space, 
brain, or both

Traumatic brain injury (TBI)
• Brain injury from trauma results from two distinct processes: Primary brain injury at time of 
trauma (cortical contusions, lacerations, bone fragmentation, diffuse axonal injury, and brainstem 
contusion); Secondary injury after initial injury from intracranial hematomas, edema, hypoxemia, 
ischemia (primarily due to elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) and/or shock), vasospasm
• Concussion is a functional manifestation of mild traumatic brain injury
• https://standardofcare.com/concussion/#:~:text=A%20sport%20related%20concussion%20is%20a%20traumatic,psychological%20implications%20in%20both%20short%20and%20long%2Dterm
• Concussion: Clinical syndrome biomechanical induced alteration of brain function (typically affects memory & orientation, may involve loss of 
consciousness)
• Typically with immediate & transient loss of consciousness with a brief period of amnesia following force to the body (including head, rapid brain 
movement causes axonal stretching and microstructural damage) that effects the brain - neurologic recovery usually complete
• Diagnosis based on history & physical: Acute Sx - Headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, blurred vision; delayed Sx - mood changes, memory issues, sleep 
disturbances
• Functional manifestation of mild traumatic brain injury, but no trauma related intracranial abnormalityis seen on CT or MRI 
• Neurometabolic cascade causes brain to be more sensitive to stress and further injury until fully recovered

Subdural hematoma (SDH)
• Located between dura and arachnoid mater
• Usually due to tearing of bridging veins (venous blood) often from head injuries (often in elderly and chronic

bleeding is greatest in first few months after initial hemorrhage
