Session Objectives
1. Explain the three interphases that seals central nervous system from the 
extracellular fluid and rest of the body
2. Identify and describe the major structural components and functional roles of the 
blood-brain barrier.
3. Explain and differentiate the anatomy of the scalp, skull, meninges, meningeal 
spaces, and mechanisms of common brain herniations.
4. Illustrate and label the morphology and key features of the ventricular system, 
including the choroid plexus and its pathologies such as hydrocephalus.
5. Analyze and predict how the blood-brain barrier, cerebrospinal fluid, and 
ventricular system operate in maintaining neural homeostasis, and assess the 
functional consequences of deficits in these structures due to injury or disease.

A patient presents with neurological symptoms secondary to 
increased blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability after a traumatic 
brain injury. Damage to which of the following proteins, essential 
in tight junctions, could disrupt BBB integrity by weakening cell-cell 
adhesion between endothelial cells?
Clinical Question
A. Connexin-43
B. Laminin
C. Zona Occludens
D. Integrin
Zona occludens proteins (ZO-1, ZO-2, ZO-3) are critical in forming and 
maintaining tight junctions in the BBB. Traumatic brain injury can 
disrupt these proteins, weakening cell-cell adhesion and increasing BBB 
permeability. This dysfunction allows immune cells and inflammatory 
molecules to enter the brain parenchyma, potentially contributing to 
neuroinflammation and worsening neurological outcomes.

Clinical Question
Levodopa, a primary treatment for Parkinson’s disease, can 
frequently cause nausea as a side effect. This is due in part to 
levodopa’s interaction with which structure in the brainstem, 
known for lacking a blood-brain barrier and for its role in 
detecting blood-borne emetic agents?
A. Nucleus Tractus Solitarius
B. Pineal Gland
C. Area Postrema
D. Subfornical Organ
The area postrema is sensitive to circulating blood-borne 
agents, such as levodopa, because it lacks a blood-brain 
barrier. This allows it to detect emetic substances directly in 
the bloodstream, often leading to nausea in patients taking 
dopaminergic medications for Parkinson’s disease.

Circulation of CSF
•
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) surrounds the brain 
and spinal cord, and may function as a shock 
absorber for the CNS. 
•
It may also serve an immunological function 
analogous to the lymphatic system (so-called 
“glymphatic”)
•
It is an ultrafiltrate of the plasma secreted by 
specialized cells that line the ventricles called 
the choroid plexus (upto 500 ml/day)
•
The CSF circulate via lateral ventricles → 
foramen of Monro→ third ventricle → sylvian 
aqueduct → fourth ventricle → foramen of 
Magendie and foramen of Magendie → 
subarachnoid space→ arachnoid granulation → 
dural sinuses.
