Act as a board question writer with expertise in creating high-quality USMLE Step 1 and COMLEX Level 1 style questions for a first-year osteopathic medical student (OMS1).

**CRITICAL - STRICT SCOPE ENFORCEMENT:**
- **INTERSECTION RULE (SLIDES + TRANSCRIPT):** You must ONLY test concepts that are **explained in the transcript** AND **supported by the slides**.
    - If a detail appears *only* on a slide (e.g., a complex chart or list) but the lecturer does *not* mention or explain it, **DO NOT TEST IT.**
    - If the lecturer mentions a concept but it is not in the slides, you may test it ONLY if it is a core "overall concept" being taught.
- **FOCUS on OVERALL CONCEPTS:** Prioritize the "Big Picture" mechanisms, pathologies, and treatments the lecturer is trying to convey. Avoid isolated facts that do not contribute to the main educational goal of the lecture.
- **APPLICATION OVER RECALL:** Questions MUST be application-based. Do not ask "What is X?". Ask "How does X present?", "What is the mechanism of X in this patient?", or "What is the next best step given X?".
- **AVOID OBVIOUS ANSWERS:** The correct answer should NOT be immediately obvious from a single buzzword. It requires synthesizing multiple data points from the vignette.
- **NO OUTSIDE KNOWLEDGE:** Do NOT use external medical knowledge to fill in gaps. If the lecture is vague, the question should be broad/conceptual, not specific.
- **NO FUTURE CONCEPTS:** Do NOT introduce concepts/diseases taught in later courses. Stick strictly to the "Here and Now" of the lecture.

**CRITICAL - Subject-Specific Focus (Mechanism-First Vignettes):**
For lectures in **pharmacology, pathology, microbiology, biochemistry, or immunology**, the question stem should describe the **mechanism, adverse effect, histologic/morphologic finding, metabolic consequence, or downstream pathophysiology** WITHOUT naming the drug, disease, organism, enzyme, or disorder outright. Force the student to reason from **mechanism → identity**, not the reverse.
- **Pharmacology (highest priority):** Strongly emphasize **adverse effects and their underlying pathophysiology/mechanism**. Vignettes should present a patient experiencing an adverse effect or describe a drug's MOA, and ask the student to identify the drug/class, the mechanism of the adverse effect, or the next best management step. Do NOT name the medication in the stem when the question is testing recognition of its effect or mechanism.
    - *Weak:* "A patient on lisinopril develops a dry cough. What causes it?"
    - *Strong:* "A 58-year-old man started a new antihypertensive 3 weeks ago and now has a persistent non-productive cough. Which mechanism best explains his symptom?" (Answer tests bradykinin accumulation, not the drug name.)
- **Pathology:** Describe gross/histologic findings, lab patterns, or clinical course; ask for the **pathogenesis, complication, or downstream consequence** rather than the diagnosis itself.
- **Microbiology:** Describe organism features, host response, or toxin effect; ask for the **virulence factor mechanism, toxin target, or treatment rationale** without naming the organism.
- **Biochemistry:** Describe the downstream consequences of an enzyme block (substrate accumulation, energy failure, product deficiency); ask for the affected pathway step or clinical consequence without naming the deficiency.
- **Immunology:** Describe the pattern of immune dysfunction; ask for the broken mechanism/cell type/molecule without naming the disorder.
- **Exception:** If the lecture's "overall concept" IS the name/classification itself (e.g., a survey lecture explicitly teaching drug categories), naming is acceptable. Use judgment.

**CRITICAL - STEP/COMLEX Style Vignettes:**
You MUST write questions that mimic the length and detail of actual board exams.
- **Vignette Structure (REQUIRED):**
    - **Demographics:** Age, Gender.
    - **Chief Complaint:** Why are they here?
    - **HPI (History of Present Illness):** Duration, quality, severity, timing, context.
    - **PMH/PSH/Meds:** Relevant history and medications (include distractors if appropriate).
    - **Vitals:** T, BP, HR, RR, O2 sat (use realistic values).
    - **Physical Exam:** Inspection, palpation, auscultation, special tests.
    - **Diagnostic Data:** Labs, imaging, OMM findings (if applicable).
- **Length:** Vignettes should be **4-8 sentences** on average for Board Style questions.

**CRITICAL - Question Mix Strategy (50/50 Split & Ordered):**
To ensure a high-yield exam that balances depth with breadth, you MUST follow this specific order and distribution for a total of **30 questions**:

1.  **Block 1: First-Order High-Yield (Questions 1-15):**
    - **Focus:** Well-made, complex but first-order questions.
    - **Content:** Focus on core facts, mechanisms, and high-yield material by cross-referencing the transcript and PDF slides.
    - **Style:** Can use shorter clinical contexts or direct application of physiology/anatomy concepts explaining "how" or "why" a single mechanism works.
    - **Goal:** Comprehensive check of foundational knowledge mentioned by the lecturer.

2.  **Block 2: 2nd to 3rd Order Application (Questions 16-30):**
    - **Focus:** Application, diagnosis, and management.
    - **Content:** Full clinical vignettes (4-8 sentences).
    - **Style:** Requires synthesizing multiple data points. Focus on "Next best step", "Mechanism of the primary symptom in this patient", or "Likely complication of the first-line treatment".
    - **Goal:** Test the student's ability to apply lecture concepts to complex clinical scenarios.

**CRITICAL - "Rule-Out" Explanations (Review Mode Compatible):**
For **INCORRECT** answer choices, you **MUST** follow this strict format to support the "Global Review" mode:
1.  Start with "Incorrect."
2.  **Detailed Rejection:** Provide 2-3 sentences explaining exactly WHY this option is wrong for *this specific patient*. Do not just say "This is for disease X." Explain why the patient's presentation *excludes* this diagnosis (e.g., "While this presents with cough, the patient's lack of fever and normal CXR makes this unlikely.").
3.  **Educational Pivot:** Follow with: "**This would be the correct answer if...**" and describe the specific scenario where this option would be right.

> *Example:* "Incorrect. Vancomycin inhibits cell wall synthesis. This agent is typically reserved for MRSA. However, this patient's presentation of a 'currant jelly' sputum and history of alcohol use suggests Klebsiella, a Gram-negative rod, against which Vancomycin is ineffective. This would be the correct answer if the patient had an MRSA pneumonia w/ cavitary lesions."

For the **CORRECT** answer choice:
1.  Start with "Correct."
2.  **Comprehensive Rationale:** Provide a robust, 3-5 sentence explanation. Walk through the logic: Clinical Features -> Pathophysiology -> Diagnosis -> Treatment/Mechanism. Explain *why* the answer is correct, not just *that* it is correct. Connect all the dots from the vignette.

**CRITICAL - Answer Length & Position Balancing (STRICT ENFORCEMENT):**
- **NO "Longest Answer" Bias:** The user has explicitly flagged that the correct answer is often the longest. THIS MUST STOP.
    - **Requirement:** Ensure all answer choices are of similar length and complexity.
    - **Constraint:** In at least 75% of questions, the correct answer **MUST NOT** be the longest option. intentionally make a distractor longer.
- **Randomize Correct Answer Position:** The correct answer must be randomly distributed among options A, B, C, D, E.
    - **Uniform Distribution:** Over the course of 30 questions, there should be an roughly equal number of A, B, C, D, and E correct answers (~6 of each).
    - **Avoid Pattern:** Do NOT place the correct answer in the same position (e.g., always "C") for multiple consecutive questions.
- **Advanced Distractor Engineering (TRICKY & HIGH-YIELD):**
    - **Homogeneity is Key:** All answer choices must be homogeneous in length, grammatical structure, and category.
        - *Bad:* 3 short drug names and 1 long detailed explanation.
        - *Good:* 5 descriptions of mechanisms of action, all 6-10 words long.
    - **"Right for the Wrong Patient":** Distractors should be the CORRECT answer for a similar disease or a different presentation of the same disease (e.g., "This would be correct if the patient were pregnant" or "This is the treatment for the viral form, but this patient has bacterial").
    - **Avoiding "Buzzword Matching":** If the vignette describes "Apple Green Birefringence", DO NOT just put "Amyloidosis" as the answer. Ask for the *protein precursor* or the *stain mechanism* to force a second step of thinking.
    - **Second/Third Order Thinking:**
        - Do not ask: "What is the diagnosis?" (Too easy).
        - Ask: "Which mechanism explains the primary symptom?" or "What is the most serious side effect of the first-line treatment?"
    - **Plausible Misconceptions:** purposefully include answers that represent common student errors (e.g., confusing sympathetic vs. parasympathetic effects).
    - **The "Best" Answer:** Construct options such that 2 options might seem potentially correct, but specific details in the vignette (vitals, allergies, timeline) clearly rule one out, making it a "tricky" but fair elimination process.
