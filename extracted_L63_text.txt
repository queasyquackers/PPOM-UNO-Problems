Headache Assessment
Purpose of assessment
- Exclude any urgent, emergent, and/or serious cause
- Make an accurate diagnosis of headache type
- Identify exacerbating/aggravating factors
- Optimize treatment (acute and preventative)
- Explain diagnosis and rationale for treatment
Assessment Tools
- History taking
- Physical exam
- Headache diary
- Imaging studies
- Laboratory analysis

Migraine Headache
• Affects 12-15% of the general population
• Female > males
- Up to 17% in females
- Up to 6% in males
• Family history (present in 70% of migraine cases)
• Aura symptoms around 5%

Migraine Headache 
- Pathophysiology -
• Primarily a neuronal dysfunction
• Initially thought to be due to dilatation of blood vessels, however proven to be incorrect
• Cortical spreading depression (Cortical Spreading Depression of Leao)
- Self-propagating wave of neuronal and glial depolarization spreading across cerebral cortex
- Cortical spreading depression leads to:
- Cause the aura of migraine
- Activate trigeminal nerve afferents
- Alter blood-brain barrier permeability

Migraine Headache: Aura
• Appears over 5 to 20 minutes and usually lasts less than 60 minutes
• Aura symptoms can be visual, sensory, or motor in nature
• Visual aura is the most common type of neurological event (90%)
--- Page 24 ---
Migraine Headache: Headache/Pain
• Typically unilateral, throbbing, moderate to severe pain, and aggravated by physical activity
• Usually gradual onset
• Pain usually peaks and then subsides:
- 4 to 72 hours in adults
- 1 to 48 hours in children
• Usually accompanied by nausea (90%) and vomiting (30%)
• Photophobia, Phonophobia, Osmophobia

Migraine Headache: Without Aura
Diagnostic Criteria
Attacks lasting 4-72 hours, having occurred at least 5 times
At least two of the following: At least one of the following:
• Unilateral
• Pulsating
• Moderate to severe
• Aggravated by exercise
• Nausea
• Photophobia
• Phonophobia

Migraine Headache: With Aura
Diagnostic Criteria
• Migraine without aura PLUS
• One or more transient focal neurological signs:
- Visual
- Sensory
- Aphasia
- Motor
• Gradual development of aura symptoms
• Aura lasts 4 to 60 minutes followed by  or accompanied 
by headache within 60 minutes

Migraine Headache
- Acute Treatment -
Basic Principles
• Correct dose
• Correct formulation
• Step-up treatment
• Simple analgesia —> combinations —> triptans/ergots 
• Early intervention
• Use at least 3 times before judging effectiveness
• Use a maximum of 2-3 days per week or 8 days in a month

Tension-Type Headache
• Most common type of headache!
• Women > men
• Mild to moderate in severity
• Genetics may play a minor role
• Lasting as little as 30 minutes to as long as 7 days
• Myofascial tenderness is increased
• Often coexists with migraine headache
• Triggers - stress, fatigue, lack of sleep
• Comorbid conditions - depression and anxiety in >50% of patients
--- Page 38 ---
Tension-Type Headache
- Pathophysiology -
• Precise mechanism is not certain, likely multifactorial
• Peripheral activation or sensitization of myofascial nociceptors - episodic
• Sensitization of pain pathways in the central nervous system due to prolonged nociceptive
stimuli from pericranial myofascial tissues - chronic
• Nitric oxide may play a role in tension-type headaches - currently under study
• Genetic factors may play a role for chronic, not episodic tension-type headache
--- Page 39 ---
Tension-Type Headache
- IHS Criteria -
• Require at least 10 episodes of headache, each lasting 30 minutes to seven days, 
which fulfill the following conditions:
At least two of the following:
- Bilateral location
- Pressing or tightening (non-pulsating) quality
- Mild or moderate intensity
- Not aggravated by routine physical activity such as walking or climbing stairs
Both of the following:
- No nausea or vomiting
- No more than one of photophobia or phonophobia

Episodic Tension-Type Headache
• Episodic TTH (<15 headaches a month) - initial treatment with simple analgesics
- NSAIDs or Aspirin
- Acetaminophen
- Parenteral Medications
- Ketorolac, Metoclopramide, Chlorpromazine
- Limited Evidence - used in severe cases
• Chronic TTH (≥15 headache days a month), for at least 6 months
- Acute symptoms - NSAIDs or acetaminophen
- Amitriptyline, skeletal muscle relaxants, fluoxetine
- Stress managements, OMT, physical therapy, massage, acupuncture

Cluster Headache
• Age of onset 25-50 years, Male > Female
• Diagnostic criteria:
- Attacks “clustered” in time (>5)
- Severe unilateral, orbital supraorbital, and/or temporal pain
- Duration of 15 to 180 minutes, occurring 1 to 8 times per day
- Associated with one of the following ipsilateral signs:
- Conjunctival injection/lacrimation
- Nasal congestion/rhinorrhea
- Eyelid edema
- Forehead/facial swelling
- Miosis/ptosis (possibly both)
- Restlessness/agitation

Cluster Headache 
- Pathophysiology -
• Complex and remains incompletely understood
• Most widely accepted theory is hypothalamic activation with secondary activation 
of the trigeminal-autonomic reflex, likely via the trigeminal-hypothalamic pathway
• Another accepted theory is neurogenic inflammation of the walls of the cavernous 
sinus obliterates venous outflow, which injures the traversing sympathetic fibers 
of the intracranial internal carotid artery and its branches
--- Page 45 ---
• Treatment
- Acute: 
- 100% Oxygen, subcutaneous or nasal triptans
- Chronic/Preventative:
- Verapamil, lithium, corticosteroids, anticonvulsants

Secondary Headache - RED FLAGS -
• Sudden onset, worst headache of my life (thunder-clap)
• Onset after age 50
• History of recent trauma
• Currently anti-coagulated 
• Systemic signs (fever, myalgias, weight loss)
• Systemic disease (malignancy, HIV)
• Headache with change in posture
• Headache with straining (valsalva, coughing)
• Change in frequency and severity
• Neurological symptoms or abnormal physical exam findings
• Altered mentation and focal neurological findings

Secondary Headaches
- Intracerebral Hemorrhage -
--- Page 55 ---
• Combination of new headache (87%), neck stiffness (83%), fever (77%), 
and altered mental state (69%) 
• Infection involving brain parenchyma (encephalitis) presents similarly 
with headache (81%), fever (90%), and altered consciousness (97%), but 
seizures are frequent (67% in encephalitis while only 5% in meningitis). 
• Pain is caused by inflammation of intracranial pain-sensitive structures 
including blood vessels at the base of the brain.
• Commonly throbbing in character, bilateral, and occipital or nuchal in 
location.

Secondary Headaches
- Lumbar Puncture -
--- Page 57 ---
• Systemic vasculitis that affects medium-sized and large arteries (temporal), especially
branches of the external carotid artery
• Inflammation of the pain-sensitive arterial wall produces headache, and arterial stenosis
leads to ischemia
• Women > men and is uncommon before 60 years of age
• Most common symptom is a new, nonspecific headache, unilateral or bilateral and is often
fairly severe and boring in quality
• ESR is elevated, but not invariably; requires prompt evaluation to avoid vision loss
• Diagnosis made by biopsy of an affected temporal artery, which is characteristically
thickened, nonpulsatile, dilated, and tender
Secondary Headaches
- Giant Cell (Temporal) Arteritis -
--- Page 58 ---
• New onset of headache in middle or late life should always raise concern
• 50% of patients with brain tumor complain of headache
• Headaches associated with brain tumors are most often nonspecific in character, mild to
moderate in severity, dull and steady in nature, and intermittent.
• Characteristically bifrontal, worse ipsilaterally, and aggravated by a change in position or
by maneuvers that increase intracranial pressure, such as coughing, sneezing, and straining
at stool.
• Classically maximal on awakening in the morning and is associated with nausea and 
vomiting.
